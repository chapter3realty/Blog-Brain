# Retirees relocating to coastal South Carolina: hopes, fears, questions and feelings

Research date: 2026-10-10. Scope: people aged 55 to 75 who move in retirement, with a focus on the Myrtle Beach area (Horry County).

## 1. Survey data: why retirees move and what they want

### Takeaway
Most adults 50+ want to stay put, but 44% expect to move, mostly because of cost. Buyers 60+ most often buy to be near family and friends, to retire, or to get a smaller home. They move a median of only 35 miles, so the long-distance coastal relocator is a minority with different worries. The Carolinas, and Myrtle Beach in particular, now top the boomer moving data.

### Cited Findings

- 75% of adults 50+ want to stay in their current home as they age. 73% want to stay in their community. [AARP press release, 2024-12-10](https://www.aarp.org/press/releases/2024-12-10-new-aarp-report-majority-adults-50-plus-age-place-policies-communities-catch-up.html)
- 44% expect they will have to move. The release names rising rent or mortgage costs (71%), property maintenance (60%) and taxes (55%) as reasons. The release does not restate the base for these three figures. ### Inferences
- Two groups of readers exist. Most 60+ buyers move locally, with a median of 35 miles. The Myrtle Beach relocator from New York, Pennsylvania or Ohio is in the long-distance minority. That reader's questions are different: what the area is like, where to find doctors, how to buy from far away.
- Family pulls both ways. "Closer to family" is the top reason for 60+ buyers. For a northern retiree who moves south, the move often takes them away from grandchildren. A story can show that tension honestly.
- Most 60+ buyers felt it was "just the right time." About 1 in 6 felt they had no choice. A story can speak to either reader: the one who chose the moment, and the one whose moment was chosen for them.
- Buyers 70+ weigh health facilities far more than younger buyers. A 72-year-old reader is likely to ask how far the hospital is before asking about the pool.

### Gaps
- No Pew Research survey on retiree relocation was found. Pew's 2025 older-adult survey was not checked for moving questions.
- Freddie Mac research on older homeowners was not reviewed within the tool budget.
- No PulteGroup or Del Webb national relocation survey from 2024 to 2026 was found. The 2010 and 2019 surveys are dated.
- Census or IRS migration data by age for Horry County was not pulled. The ACS flows used in section 5 cover all ages.

## 2. Fears and regrets

### Takeaway
The documented fears are money lasting, health care costs and access, losing friends, leaving family, and the work and grief of sorting a lifetime of belongings. Social loneliness tends to rise after a long move. Emotional loneliness does not, when a spouse comes along. Regret data is thin. The "halfback" pattern of Florida retirees moving north to the Carolinas is documented, with cost, insurance and distance from family as the reported reasons.

### Cited Findings

Money and fixed income
- EBRI/Greenwald 2025 Retirement Confidence Survey (fetched; n=2,767, online, January 2 to February 3, 2025; retirees n=1,005):
  - 78% of retirees are confident they will have enough money to live comfortably, up from 74%.
  - Nearly half agree they spend less than they could because they worry about running out of money.
  - Retirees who said expenses were higher than expected: overall spending 45% (down from 51% in 2024), health or dental 38%, taxes 37%, housing 35%.
  - 80% worry about changes that would reduce Social Security. 78% worry about cuts to Medicare.
  - 7 in 10 say they are having the retirement lifestyle they envisioned. 26% say it is better than expected.
  [EBRI/Greenwald 2025 RCS report PDF](https://greenwaldresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2025-rcs-release-report.pdf); [EBRI release](https://www.ebri.org/content/35th-annual-retirement-confidence-survey-reports-worker-confidence-unchanged--while-retirees-feeling-better)
  - Note: one search summary said "40% of retirees" found health costs higher than expected. The report figure is 38%.
- CNBC/SurveyMonkey 2024: 56% of retirees are very or somewhat concerned about running out of savings. 53% say they did not save enough. [SurveyMonkey/CNBC](https://www.surveymonkey.com/curiosity/cnbc-retirement-2024/)
- AARP 2024: cost is the main reason 50+ adults expect to move. The release names rent or mortgage 71%, maintenance 60% and taxes 55%. [AARP press release](https://www.aarp.org/press/releases/2024-12-10-new-aarp-report-majority-adults-50-plus-age-place-policies-communities-catch-up.html)

Loneliness and making friends
- University of Michigan National Poll on Healthy Aging, 2024 (search summary only; the primary page returned 403). Published in JAMA.
  - About 33% of adults 50 to 80 felt lonely some of the time or often. About 29% felt isolated.
  - Adults 50 to 64 were more likely than those 65 to 80 to feel lonely or isolated.
  [Michigan Medicine Health Lab](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/1-3-older-adults-still-experience-loneliness-and-isolation); [IHPI](https://ihpi.umich.edu/news/loneliness-and-isolation-back-pre-pandemic-levels-still-high-older-adults)
- Savaş et al., "Trouble in paradise? Emotional and social loneliness among international retirement migrants," Psychology and Aging, 2025 (search summary only).
  - The sample was 4,995 Dutch retirees who had moved to 40 countries and 1,338 who stayed home, aged 66 to 90.
  - The movers were socially lonelier but not emotionally lonelier.
  - Losing touch with good friends at home raised both kinds of loneliness.
  - More contact with neighbors and a sense of belonging lowered both.
  [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/health/2025/03/13/thinking-of-retiring-abroad-you-might-be-lonelier-than-if-you-stayed-home-new-study-sugges); [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/a-to-z-health/senior-health/retiring-abroad-may-lead-to-more-loneliness-study-finds)
- Australian study of 83 people moving into retirement communities, 2015 (search summary only): loneliness fell and neighbor support grew after the move. Contact with old friends fell. [Flinders University research record](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/adapting-to-retirement-community-life-changes-in-social-networks-/)
- Del Webb resident survey (search summary only; the BusinessWire and Builder pages returned 403). This is a company survey of its own residents.
  - Over 1,300 residents aged 55+ in 14 states took part.
  - 91% feel socially connected. 71% say where they live makes it easier to form meaningful connections.
  - More than half became more socially engaged after moving in.
  - BusinessWire copies carry 2021 and 2023 dates.
  [BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20231206058058/en); [Builder](https://www.builderonline.com/?p=173370)

Downsizing possessions

### Inferences
- Money fear is quiet, not loud. Most retirees say they are confident, yet nearly half hold back spending out of worry. A story can show the second check of the budget, not panic.
- The new costs a northern buyer may not expect are insurance and flood coverage, not property tax. South Carolina's property tax rate is low. Wind, hail and flood coverage are separate and location-specific. The site must not state a premium or payment amount (repo rule). A story can show the surprise and the question without a number.
- Loneliness after a long move is likely to be the social kind: no one to call for lunch. A spouse does not fix that. The research points to one close friend, neighbor contact and a sense of belonging as the things that help.
- Downsizing is the most emotional task in the move. It is often late and rushed, and families argue over it.

- No survey was found that measures fear of being taken advantage of by sellers or agents. These were not checked against ic3.gov because the PDF did not download. Do not use them without verification.
- No survey was found on HOA rules as a fear for retiree buyers. Florida HOA and condo fees appear only as a broker-reported reason for leaving Florida.
- No reliable regret rate exists beyond the HireAHelper user survey.
- No current County Health Rankings figure for Horry County was retrieved.
- No survey specific to hurricane anxiety among retirees moving to the Carolinas was found.

## 3. Turning points and relief after the move

### Takeaway
The research shows a common arc. First comes ambivalence and delay. Then a trigger: retirement, a health event, the death of a partner, a grandchild, or cost. Then the hard work of sorting and leaving. Relief comes later. People report pride and a sense of empowerment once the downsizing is done. Loneliness eases once they have one close friend, contact with neighbors, and a feeling of belonging. Gerontology names the end state "residential normalcy": a home that feels comfortable and that the person can manage.

### Cited Findings
- Delay is normal. Very old people (80 to 89) often postpone the decision, a pattern the authors call "a 'transfer of the horizon.'" Some hand the decision to their children: "That is up to my children to decide." [Löfqvist et al. 2013](https://lucris.lub.lu.se/ws/files/1369832/4022872.pdf)
- Triggers they named include loneliness after friends or a partner die, health decline, and pressure from others. "I feel lonely, and that is why I think I should move, but I don't want to really." [Löfqvist et al. 2013](https://lucris.lub.lu.se/ws/files/1369832/4022872.pdf)
- Reasons to move "reflect the urge to maintain independent, to stay in control and avoid loneliness." Reasons not to move include "fear of losing continuity of habits and routines." [Löfqvist et al. 2013, abstract](https://lucris.lub.lu.se/ws/files/1369832/4022872.pdf)
- Triggers can be anticipated, like a change in preferred lifestyle, or abrupt critical events. They work as push or pull factors. [Perry, Andersen and Kaplan 2014, The Gerontologist](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4118138)
- Older adults plan ahead. Koss and Ekerdt found that retirees aged 67 to 97 (n=30) "are continually thinking about, 'Where is the best place that I should grow old?'" They reason either pre-emptively (this home will work) or contingently (a future move to a named place). [KU News, 2017](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2017/09/13/aging-populations-often-thoroughly-contemplate-housing-decisions-researchers-find)
- After downsizing: Ekerdt's book draws on more than 100 households. Despite fatigue and emotional strain, people report satisfaction at having finished, and they feel empowered afterward. (Search summary only.) [Columbia University Press blog](https://cupblog.org/?p=43585); [KU News, 2020](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2020/05/18/downsizing-book-encourages-older-people-confront-their-possessions)
- What eases loneliness after a move: at least one close friend in the new place, contact with neighbors, and a sense of belonging. Keeping contact with people back home also helps. [Euronews, on Savaş et al. 2025](https://www.euronews.com/health/2025/03/13/thinking-of-retiring-abroad-you-might-be-lonelier-than-if-you-stayed-home-new-study-sugges)
- In a 2015 Australian study, loneliness fell and neighbor support rose after a move into a retirement community. [Flinders University](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/adapting-to-retirement-community-life-changes-in-social-networks-/)
- Del Webb residents (company survey): more than half said they became more socially engaged after moving in. [Builder](https://www.builderonline.com/?p=173370)
- Golant's residential normalcy model (search summary only):
  - People do best when a home gives "residential comfort" (pleasant, hassle-free, memorable) and "residential mastery" (competent and in control).
  - When both are positive, the person has reached residential normalcy.
  - Many people resist moving even when a move would help.
  [Residential Normalcy in Later Life, Hasselt University](https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/bitstream/1942/46092/1/Residential%20Normalcy%20in%20Later%20Life%20%20A%20Life%20Course%20Perspective.pdf); [Golant, Aging in the Right Place](https://www.healthpropress.com/product/aging-in-the-right-place)
- After the purchase: 7 in 10 retirees say they are having the retirement lifestyle they envisioned. 26% say it is better than they expected. [EBRI/Greenwald 2025 RCS](https://greenwaldresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2025-rcs-release-report.pdf)
- The buying process itself: about a quarter of buyers 60+ said no step was difficult (26% of ages 60-69, 24% of ages 70-78). For those who found a step hard, it was most often finding the right property (55% for both groups). Paperwork was hard for 15%. [NAR PDF, Exhibit 3-5](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)
- What buyers 60+ want most from an agent: help finding the right home (55% of ages 60-69, 57% of ages 70-78). Help negotiating terms was 13% and 10%. Help on price was 10% and 9%. [NAR PDF, Exhibit 4-2](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- The moments that carry feeling are:
  - The decision finally made after months of "not yet."
  - The last walk through the old house.
  - Choosing what to keep.
  - The first morning in the new home.
  - The first time a neighbor knocks.
  - The first grandchild visit to the new house.
  - The first storm season, and finding it was manageable.
  These follow from the findings above. They are not quotes from Myrtle Beach buyers.
- Relief in the research is about control and comfort: "I can manage this house," "I know where the hospital is," "I have someone to call." A story that ends there matches the evidence. A story that ends on "paradise" does not.
- Buyers 60+ value finding the right home over negotiation help. A story about how the right house was found fits what they say they want.

### Gaps
- No US qualitative study of coastal amenity migrants' first-year experience was found within the tool budget.
- Journalism with direct retiree quotes about relief after moving to Myrtle Beach is mostly on robots-blocked local sites. None was read.
- The story bank (stories/stories.json) is the only allowed source of real client moments. This research gives themes, not stories.

## 4. Academic research on retirement migration and late-life move decisions

### Takeaway
Litwak and Longino's 1987 developmental model is the base theory. It describes three kinds of late-life moves: at retirement, at moderate disability, and at major chronic disability. Coastal amenity moves at 55 to 75 are mostly the first kind. Later work adds that the decision is ambivalent and often postponed. Older adults plan for a possible decline. Possessions are a major barrier. A home is judged on comfort and on control.

### Cited Findings
- Litwak, E. and Longino, C. F. (1987). "Migration Patterns Among the Elderly: A Developmental Perspective." The Gerontologist 27(3): 266 to 272. It places elderly migration in a developmental frame, with three kinds of moves tied to health: at retirement, at moderate disability, and at major chronic disability. [DOI 10.1093/geront/27.3.266](https://doi.org/10.1093/geront/27.3.266); [abstract record](https://research-portal.najah.edu/migrant/27348/)
- Tests of the model:
  - A 2011 Journal of Applied Gerontology study using 2005 to 2007 ACS data found Florida's later-life migration flows broadly matched it. [Record](https://research-portal.najah.edu/migrant/25423/)
  - A Dutch study found no evidence of the proposed sequence of moves. [International Journal of Ageing and Later Life](https://journal.ep.liu.se/IJAL/article/view/1175)
- Perry, Andersen and Kaplan (2014), "Relocation Remembered," The Gerontologist 54(1). It reviews Lawton and Nahemow's environmental press theory, Wiseman's behavioral model of relocation decisions, and Litwak and Longino. It calls for an updated view of housing transitions. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4118138)
- Silverstein and Zablotsky (1996), J Gerontol B 51(3): retirement-community migration in middle and late old age is driven by social and amenity reasons and also by the need for assisted living. (Search summary only.) [Abstract](https://psychsoc.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/51/3/S150)
- Duncombe, Robbins and Wolf (2003), J Gerontol B 58(4): fiscal factors such as taxes and services relate to whether retirement-age people stay in or move to a place. (Search summary only.) [Abstract](https://psychsoc.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/58/4/S244)
- Löfqvist, Granbom, Himmelsbach, Iwarsson, Oswald and Haak (2013), "Voices on Relocation and Aging in Place in Very Old Age: A Complex and Ambivalent Matter," The Gerontologist 53(6): 919 to 927. 80 people aged 80 to 89 in Sweden and Germany. It finds that relocation reasoning is "a complex and ambivalent matter." [Author manuscript](https://lucris.lub.lu.se/ws/files/1369832/4022872.pdf); [DOI 10.1093/geront/gnt034](https://doi.org/10.1093/geront/gnt034)
- Koss and Ekerdt (2017), "Residential Reasoning and the Tug of the Fourth Age," The Gerontologist 57: 921 to 929. 30 US retirees aged 67 to 97. Housing choices are deliberate and look ahead to possible decline. [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2017/09/13/aging-populations-often-thoroughly-contemplate-housing-decisions-researchers-find)
- Ekerdt et al. (2004), "Household Disbandment in Later Life," J Gerontol B 59(5): S265. Ekerdt (2020), Downsizing: Confronting Our Possessions in Later Life, Columbia University Press. Ekerdt's term "material convoy" names the belongings that follow a person through life. [J Gerontol B abstract](https://psychsoc.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/59/5/S265); [KU News](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2020/05/18/downsizing-book-encourages-older-people-confront-their-possessions)
- Golant's residential normalcy model: comfort and mastery. See section 3. [Hasselt University paper](https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/bitstream/1942/46092/1/Residential%20Normalcy%20in%20Later%20Life%20%20A%20Life%20Course%20Perspective.pdf)
- Savaş et al. (2025), Psychology and Aging 40(4): retirement migrants are socially lonelier but not emotionally lonelier. See section 2. [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/a-to-z-health/senior-health/retiring-abroad-may-lead-to-more-loneliness-study-finds)

### Inferences
- A 55 to 75 buyer moving to the coast is usually making Litwak and Longino's first move: healthy, by choice, for lifestyle. Many readers are also thinking about the second move, toward support or family. A story can acknowledge "where will we be at 85?" without fear-selling.
- The ambivalence research suggests a story should not shame delay. Readers who have waited years are normal.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study specific to retirement migration to coastal South Carolina was found.
- Golant's 2014 Gerontologist paper and the Silverstein, Duncombe and Savaş papers were not read in full. Their findings come from abstracts or summaries.
- No study measuring anxiety levels in late-life moves was found within the budget.

## 5. Myrtle Beach and Horry County specifics

### Takeaway
The Myrtle Beach metro has the fastest-growing 65+ population in the country. Census figures show it up 23.1% from 2020 to 2023, to 107,430, and AP reports another 6.3% in 2024. Movers into Horry County come mostly from North Carolina, New York, Pennsylvania, Florida, Maryland, Virginia, New Jersey and Ohio. Local reporting and planning point to traffic and infrastructure, flooding, insurance and doctor access as the strains. Little first-hand newcomer reporting could be read because of robots blocks.

### Cited Findings
Growth
- Census Bureau (fetched; Wilder and Mackun, June 27, 2024; estimates April 2020 to July 2023):
  - The Myrtle Beach-Conway-North Myrtle Beach metro had the fastest-growing 65+ population of any US metro, up 23.1% to 107,430.
  - It was the second-fastest growing metro in total population.
  - Its working-age population grew 11.1%.
  - Nationally, the 65+ population grew 9.4%.
  [Census Bureau story](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2024/06/metro-areas-population-age.html)
