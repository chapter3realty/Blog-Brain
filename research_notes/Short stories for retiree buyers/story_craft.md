# The craft of very short stories (5 to 10 sentences) in nonfiction and marketing

Scope: how to make a story of 5 to 10 sentences hold a reader and stay in memory. Gathered 2026-10-10 for short stories on a real estate blog for retirees. Almost everything below is expert opinion from writers, teachers and consultants. Only three items are tested evidence: the identifiable-victim study (Small, Loewenstein and Slovic, 2007), the tappers-and-listeners study (Newton, 1990) and the narrative transportation studies (Green and Brock, 2000). Each finding says which kind it is where that matters.

House note: this repo bans em dashes, so source lines use "Source:" instead of a dash. Quotes are the authors' words. Where a quote was only available through a summary, the line says so.

## 1. Structures that work at very short length: story spine, Pixar's rules, but/therefore, the anecdotal lede and the nut graf

### Takeaway
Every short structure the sources give comes down to the same three moves: show the normal, break it with one event, show what is different after. The story spine and the South Park writers add one test: each step must follow from the one before ("because of that", "but", "therefore"), not just come after it ("and then"). In journalism the anecdote is the close-up, and the nut graf that follows it says why it matters. A person brought in at the start should come back at the end.

### Cited Findings
**Kenn Adams' story spine (expert practice, not tested)**
- Adams describes the spine as eight sentences whose openings are fixed and whose endings the storyteller fills in: "once upon a time, every day, but one day, because of that, because of that, because of that, until finally, and ever since then." He says "the beginning of the sentences are preplanned, and then the remaining part of the sentences are improvised." Source: [NPR interview by Scott Detrow, published on NHPR, 2026-06-23](https://www.nhpr.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories)
- He built it in the late 1980s while working with an improv company in New York and teaching a middle school writing workshop: "I needed some type of tool for the kids to use in order to outline their work." Source: [NHPR / NPR, 2026-06-23](https://www.nhpr.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories)
- It reached Pixar through Rebecca Stockley, an improviser who learned it in Adams' workshop. "Rebecca got hired by Pixar to teach improvisation to their writers," and she taught them "a whole bunch of stuff, including the Story Spine." Source: [NHPR / NPR, 2026-06-23](https://www.nhpr.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories)
- Adams' worked example opens: "Once upon a time, there was a girl named Dorothy, who was whisked away to the magical land of Oz." Source: [NHPR / NPR, 2026-06-23](https://www.nhpr.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories)

**Emma Coats' "Pixar rules" (one artist's tips, not tested and not an official studio rulebook)**
- Emma Coats, a former Pixar storyboard artist, posted the 22 tips as tweets over two months in 2011. The rules below are paraphrased in the reprint. Source: [RedShark News, Patrick Jong Taylor, 2013-03-12](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling)
- Rule 4 is the story spine: a setup ("once upon a time"), a routine ("every day"), a disruption ("one day"), a chain of "because of that" events, and "until finally". Source: [RedShark News](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling)
- Other rules that matter at short length, as paraphrased by the reprint: (1) we admire a character more for trying than for winning; (5) simplify, focus, merge characters, cut detours; (7) settle the ending before the middle; (13) give characters opinions, because passive characters lose the audience; (16) make the stakes clear and say what is lost if the character fails; (19) a coincidence that gets a character into trouble is fine, one that gets them out is cheating; (22) find the story's essence and its most economical telling, then build out. Source: [RedShark News](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling)

**"But" and "therefore", not "and then" (expert opinion)**
- Trey Parker, at an NYU writing class on 2014-03-06: "What should happen between every beat that you've written down, is either the word 'therefore' or 'but'." Matt Stone: "this happened, and then this happened, and then this happens -- that's not a movie." Stone on "but" and "therefore": "that gives you the causation between each beat, and that's a story." Source: [Speakola transcript](https://speakola.com/arts/matt-stone-trey-parker-nyu-writing-class-2014)

**The anecdotal lede and the nut graf (journalism practice)**
- Chip Scanlan: "The nut graf tells the reader what the writer is up to; it delivers a promise of the story's content and message." It is named because, "like a nut, it contains the 'kernel,' or essential theme, of the story." Ken Wells: "a paragraph that says what this whole story is about and why you should read it." Source: [Poynter, Chip Scanlan, 2003-05-19](https://poynter.org/?p=11371)
- Scanlan's paired examples. Julia Malone's anecdote shows crews blasting "through solid rock" to build a two-mile road to nowhere; the nut graf then says the project "shows how a little 'pork' tucked into a federal transportation bill can buy a whole hog for a community." A student story opens on two residents hit by water guns; the nut graf follows: "Berry and Lee are victims of a new urban weapon in South St. Petersburg." Source: [Poynter, 2003](https://poynter.org/?p=11371)
- The failure Scanlan names: a character in the lede who "is never seen or heard from again." His own preteen-dieting story avoids it by coming back to the girl, Sarah, near the end. Source: [Poynter, 2003](https://poynter.org/?p=11371)
- James B. Stewart, quoted by Scanlan, warns against giving away the ending in the nut graf, to keep "every bit of the suspense and curiosity so carefully cultivated in the lead." Source: [Poynter, 2003](https://poynter.org/?p=11371)
- A CUNY writing coach says the nut graf should answer "Who cares?" and "Why is this important?" Source: [CUNY Write Stuff, 2010](https://writestuff.journalism.cuny.edu/2010/09/08/heres-the-nut-of-the-matter/)
- Nieman Storyboard's collection of narrative advice. Roy Wenzl calls the usual feature "an anecdotal lede followed by summary news nut graf" and says: "When you write about things in the abstract, it's very difficult for readers to get engaged with it." Laurie Hertzel: scenes "should unfold moment by moment" and "You want the reader to feel like he's right there with you." Kiera Feldman: "I needed to start the piece mid-scene." Rebecca Allen: a narrative "lets the story unfold through character, scene and action," "usually without summing up the story and telling readers what it's about." Source: [Nieman Storyboard, "Storyboard 75", Paige Williams, 2013-10-03](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=1093)
- John Hersey, as quoted there: "If what you leave out is essential, then the details you choose to leave in must be essential." Source: [Nieman Storyboard, 2013](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=1093)

### Inferences
- The spine's 8 sentences fit the 5 to 10 sentence target almost exactly. For a blog story, "every day" is the reader's present life (the routine), "but one day" is the event that started the move, the "because of that" lines are the consequences, and "ever since then" is the new life. Several sources cut the spine to one "because of that" line; at 5 to 7 sentences that is the practical version.
- The anecdote plus nut graf pair fits a blog section well: the story is the close-up, and the next paragraph (outside the story) says what it means for the reader. That keeps the point out of the story itself (see section 8, "moral stated outright").
- Scanlan's rule matters for a short story inside a long page. If a section opens with a couple, the section or the page should come back to them once.
- Parker and Stone's rule is a quick edit test: read the story aloud and put "and then", "but" or "therefore" between each sentence. If only "and then" fits, the story is a list.

### Gaps
- I did not find AP guidance on anecdotal ledes. The AP Stylebook is paywalled, and no free AP page on the subject came up.
- Adams' own labels for the spine's parts (routine, event, consequences, climax, resolution) are not in the NPR piece. I did not reach his book.
- I did not find Coats' original tweets. The rule wording above is a reprint's paraphrase.
- Nieman Storyboard has no single how-to page on anecdotal ledes that I could find. Its view comes from the collected quotes above.

## 2. Matthew Dicks, Storyworthy: the five-second moment, stakes, starting near the end, but and therefore, the elephant, the crumbs

### Takeaway
Dicks builds every story backward from one small moment of change (the "five-second moment"). The opening is the opposite of that moment. Everything else exists to raise the stakes on the way there. He prefers small moments to big ones, cuts hard, compresses time, and never adds anything that did not happen. All of this is the opinion of a many-time Moth StorySLAM champion and teacher, not tested research.

### Cited Findings
- Dicks: "a story is about a singular moment in your life." The change is either a transformation ("I was once one person and now I'm another") or a realization. Source: [Art of Manliness interview](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)
- "I always tell people to start with the end of their story first." Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)
- The opening is the opposite of the ending. His example: if a story ends with you realizing your mother was right, it opens with you believing she was giving bad advice. Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)
- Start in motion, as films do, and skip lists and background. Do not announce "the craziest story", because it sets up a promise the story rarely meets. Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)
- Small beats big: "people can't connect to those big moments." His own example is small: a woman picks up keys he dropped at the gym, he feels small for his own selfishness, and he gives up a close parking spot. Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)
- On length: "the shorter story wins." Cut what is not needed and compress time, but never add events that did not happen. Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)
- Stakes are what keep the listener wondering; his example is the dinosaurs in Jurassic Park. The "backpack": the listener is told the character's plan, and the plan then fails piece by piece, so the listener feels the frustration. Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)
- Reader notes on the book list five stakes devices: the Elephant ("introduce some 'elephant in the room' that everyone in the audience is aware of"), the Backpack ("tell the audience the plan ahead of time"), Breadcrumbs ("hinting at what comes next"), the Hourglass ("at the moment in a story right before the most important 5 seconds... slow everything down") and the Crystal Ball ("imagining a proposed future that won't come true"). The same notes on Dicks's other rules: "'But' and 'therefore' are better, because they help keep the audience on their toes"; "tell a story like you would at a dinner table"; and "if your story ends with a moment of transformation, you want the start to be the opposite of that transformation." These are a reader's notes (Calvin French-Owen), not the book text. Source: [calv.info, Storyworthy notes](https://calv.info/bookshelf/storyworthy). A plot summary adds that the Elephant should come early, in the first minute or two. Source: [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/storyworthy/summary/)
- "But and therefore": a review of Storyworthy reports that Dicks says story beats should connect through "buts" and "therefores", not "ands", and notes the South Park creators use the same rule. It also reports his method for telling: memorize only the first and last lines and the order of scenes, and tie each scene to a place and time. Source: [Ribbonfarm, Venkatesh Rao, 2021-06-24](https://ribbonfarm.com/2021/06/24/storytelling-matthew-dicks/)
- Finding stories: "Homework for Life", a nightly note of the day's one story-worthy moment, and the "first, last, best, worst" prompts. Source: [Ribbonfarm](https://ribbonfarm.com/2021/06/24/storytelling-matthew-dicks/); [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)
- The "Dinner Test" is one of his named tools (listed as a topic in an interview; the reader notes above define it as telling the story the way you would at a dinner table). Source: [Lenny's Newsletter episode notes](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-tell-better-stories-matthew)

### Inferences
- For a retiree buyer story, the five-second moment is usually small: the first morning they walked to the mailbox without a coat, the moment they saw the one-story floor plan and stopped worrying about stairs. Dicks's "opposite beginning" then gives the first sentence: the stairs, the cold, the worry.
- Dicks's "never add events that did not happen" matches the repo rule (CLAUDE.md, `stories/stories.json`: never add a detail). Compression (cutting and joining time) is allowed by Dicks; invention is not.
- At 5 to 10 sentences there is room for one stakes device at most. The Elephant (say the problem in sentence one or two) is the one that fits. A Backpack (state the plan, then show it fail) can fit in 7 to 10 sentences.

### Gaps
- I could not see the book text. The definitions of the Elephant, Breadcrumbs ("crumbs"), Hourglasses, Crystal Balls, "but and therefore" and the Dinner Test come from a reader's notes, a review and a plot summary, which agree with each other. Dicks's own wording for them was not available.

## 3. Kindra Hall, Stories That Stick: four components, normal / explosion / new normal, the customer story

### Takeaway
Hall's four components are an identifiable character, authentic emotion, a specific moment (time and place) and specific details. Her structure is normal, explosion, new normal. Her customer story puts a real buyer, not the product, at the center. Sources disagree on the exact name of the third component. Her claims rest on her experience and on audience demonstrations, not controlled studies.

### Cited Findings
- Four components, from Hall's interview: an identifiable character (one to three people the audience can picture, not "the company"); authentic emotion (small real feelings such as hope, guilt, frustration or embarrassment work as well as big ones); a moment (bring the audience to a specific time and place, "zoom[ing] all the way in"); and specific details. Source: [Social Media Examiner interview with Kindra Hall](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/creating-stories-that-stick-how-marketers-can-win-with-story-kindra-hall/)
- On details: "Don't make them work too hard." Often one detail is enough. Her demonstration: she mentions a silver Grand Am in a story where the car does not matter, and about 95% of her audience recalled it 30 minutes later. This is a stage demonstration she reports, not a controlled study. Source: [Social Media Examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/creating-stories-that-stick-how-marketers-can-win-with-story-kindra-hall/)
- A detail works by letting the listener build the picture themselves (a "co-creative process"). Her steakhouse example: when she describes sitting across from her husband in a booth under dim lights, she asks the audience whether they were "right there in the booth with us," and hands go up. Source: [Social Media Examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/creating-stories-that-stick-how-marketers-can-win-with-story-kindra-hall/)
- A second summary lists the components as a character you are rooting for, concrete details, genuine emotion and "a turning point"; it says stories without one "drift aimlessly." It also says the product being sold does not count as a character. Source: [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=101525). This conflicts on the fourth component with the interview above, which says "a moment". Podcast show notes list it as "Place and time: 'a moment'", which agrees with the interview. Source: [Learning Leader show notes](https://learningleader.com/?p=4639)
- Structure: the normal (the world before, which the audience can relate to), the explosion (the moment that divides before and after, good or bad), the new normal (how life looks different after). Source: [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=101525). Hall's own slide deck uses the same three labels under "Crafting the Story". Source: [Kindra Hall slide deck hosted by Lockton](https://lockbox.lockton.com/asset/b293d2ba-fc9d-4d49-8b27-c68bdfce9535/Strategic-Storytelling-for-Impact.pdf)
- The book's four story types are the Value story, Founder story, Purpose story and Customer story. Source: [Prezent blog summary](https://www.prezent.ai/blog/storytelling-in-business-kindra-hall)

### Inferences
- Hall's three-part structure is the story spine with fewer parts. For 5 sentences: 1 to 2 sentences of normal, 1 sentence of explosion, 1 to 2 of new normal.
- "One detail is enough" is the key rule at this length. A story of 5 to 10 sentences can carry one concrete, picturable detail (the Grand Am, the booth). More details compete with each other.
- Hall's "the product is not a character" lines up with Miller's "your brand is not the hero" (section 5). In a customer story on a brokerage blog, the buyer is the character and the company is at most a helper in one sentence.

### Gaps
- I could not read the book's customer-story chapter, so how Hall builds a customer story step by step is from summaries only.
- The third component's exact name in the book ("a moment" versus "a turning point") is unresolved. Two sources say a specific moment in time and place; one says a turning point.

## 4. Chip and Dan Heath, Made to Stick: SUCCESs, the curse of knowledge, the challenge, connection and creativity plots

### Takeaway
The Heaths' six traits of sticky ideas are Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional and Stories. The main enemy is the curse of knowledge: once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it, so experts write abstractly. Stories work because they give both "simulation" (how to act) and "inspiration" (a reason to act). The book's framework is expert synthesis; two studies it relies on (tappers and listeners, the identifiable victim) are tested.

### Cited Findings
- The six principles, as a 2007 review summarizes them: find the core of the message (simple); "Root the message in something Unexpected, to grab your audience's attention"; use concrete evidence; "Ask yourself, Will anyone believe me?" (credible); "Tug at Emotions to make people care"; use stories. Source: [Stanford Social Innovation Review, Judith Samuelson, Summer 2007](https://ssir.org/books/reviews/entry/review_made_to_stick)
- On the curse of knowledge, the same review: "The more you know, the more challenging it is to develop and communicate a sticky message." It notes many nonprofit websites are long on information and statistics but have nothing sticky. Source: [SSIR, 2007](https://ssir.org/books/reviews/entry/review_made_to_stick)
- The Heaths' own HBR article, "The Curse of Knowledge", applies it to executives: "Top executives have had years of immersion in the logic and conventions of business," so their abstract statements summarize concrete data that frontline staff never saw. Source: [Harvard Business Review, December 2006](https://hbr.org/2006/12/the-curse-of-knowledge)
- Chip Heath on product designers: they are experts cursed by their knowledge and "can't imagine what it's like to be as ignorant as the rest of us." Source: [Fast Company, "Leadership: Ignorant Expertise"](https://www.fastcompany.com/661686/leadership-ignorant-expertise)
- Tested evidence: in 1990 Stanford doctoral student Elizabeth Newton had "tappers" tap well-known songs for "listeners". Of 120 songs, listeners named 3 (2.5%); tappers had predicted listeners would get it at least 50% of the time. The Heaths use this as their demonstration of the curse. Source: [Wagonheim summary attributing the account to the Heaths](https://wagonheim.com/the-tappers-and-the-listeners/)
- The book: "The story's power, then, is twofold: It provides simulation (knowledge about how to act) and inspiration (motivation to act)." (p. 206, as quoted in an academic review). Source: [Journal article quoting Made to Stick, University of Minnesota journals](https://pubs.lib.umn.edu/index.php/jcotr/article/download/2700/2108/9491)
- Three plots that inspire: the Challenge plot (an underdog beats long odds; the book's example is Jared Fogle, the Subway dieter), the Connection plot (a relationship that bridges a gap) and the Creativity plot (a breakthrough or a new way of solving a problem). Source: [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/storytelling-communication/)
- Tested evidence on emotion and numbers: Small, Loewenstein and Slovic (2007) ran four field experiments. People gave more when they read about one named victim (Rokia, a seven-year-old girl in Mali) than when they read statistics about millions of hungry people. Adding the statistics to Rokia's story lowered giving to her and did not raise giving overall. Source: [Stanford GSB, 2007-06-27](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/increase-charitable-donations-appeal-heart-not-head)
- Deborah Small: "It's hard for humans to generate feelings toward statistics." "When a victim has been identified, she becomes her own frame of reference." "people pay greater attention and have stronger emotional reactions to vivid rather than pallid information." Source: [Knowledge at Wharton, 2007-06-27](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/understanding-emotional-appeals-how-to-get-chartiable-donations/)
- A 2024 replication and extension of the 2007 study was published in Collabra: Psychology. I did not read its results. Source: [HKU repository record](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/348385)

### Inferences
- For a retiree blog, the Connection plot (a gap closed between people, such as moving near grandchildren) and the Challenge plot (a hard move made manageable) fit most real buyer stories. The Creativity plot fits stories about a clever solution (for example a floor plan that solved a mobility problem).
- The Rokia result supports the repo's rule P2 (few numbers, round ones). Inside a story, a number pulls the reader out of the person and into analysis. Keep numbers in the paragraph after the story, if at all.
- The curse of knowledge explains why agent-written stories drift into jargon (a builder's model name, a zone code). A story should name only things the reader can picture, which matches repo rule P4.

### Gaps
- The Heaths' book text and free chapter are behind a registration wall on heathbrothers.com. Their own definitions of each principle and of the three plots come here from a review and summaries.
- The per-condition donation amounts often quoted from the Rokia study (for example $2.38 versus $1.14) were not in any page I could read. Do not use them without the original paper.
- Whether the Heaths present the Rokia study in Made to Stick was not confirmed from a page I read.

## 5. Donald Miller, StoryBrand: customer as hero, brand as guide, three levels of problem, failure, success

### Takeaway
Miller's rule for marketing stories: the customer is the hero and the company is the guide. A guide shows empathy and authority and gives a plan. The problem works on three levels (external, internal, philosophical), and the story needs clear stakes (what failure looks like) and a picture of success. This is a consultancy's framework; its success claims are self-reported.

### Cited Findings
- "Your brand is not the hero of the story. Your customer is." "the fact that your grandfather started the company isn't as interesting as you might think. What people really care about is their own story." Source: [Donald Miller, "Your Brand Is Not the Hero", StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)
- The guide: "In stories, characters do not solve their own problems." "A good guide has two basic qualities: they empathize with the character's fears and insecurities; and they have the authority and experience to help them win the day." Source: [StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)
- Three levels of problem: "First, there's the external problem, such as a bomb that needs to be disarmed. But that external problem has to manifest an internal problem. Does the hero have what it takes to disarm the bomb? ... And lastly, there should be a philosophical problem in play, something like good vs evil. It's these three layers of problems that give the story depth and meaning." Source: [StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)
- The plan can be small: "as simple as a paradigm shift, you used to think this way, but I want you to think another way." (Punctuation adjusted; the original uses a dash.) Source: [StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)
- Stakes and failure: "If we don't know what failure might befall the hero, audiences zone out and lose interest." "A story is given drama only when there's something at stake." Source: [StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)
- Success: Miller's Allstate example shows "how good life can be when you're not worried", the "successful ending" a customer can have. Source: [StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)
- Summaries of the book state Miller's claim that companies sell solutions to external problems but customers buy solutions to internal ones: an external problem creates frustration, and the frustration drives the purchase. Source: [Shortform, "Types of Problems"](https://www.shortform.com/blog/types-of-problems/)
- Miller's own results claims (revenue tripled in 18 months, clients who "quadrupled their revenue") are self-reported marketing, not evidence. Source: [StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)

### Inferences
- The three levels map well to a retiree move. External: the house has stairs and a big yard. Internal: worry about falling, or feeling tied down. Philosophical: after a working life, a person should be able to spend their days on what they enjoy. A short story can show the external problem in scene and let the internal one show in one detail; the philosophical level is best left unsaid.
- "Brand as guide" limits the company to one sentence inside a short story, and it must be something the company did that the story bank records. The repo already forbids "we helped" and "our client" (STANDARD T2) and requires the company, not a person, to speak (C7).
- Miller's "failure" device needs care on a retiree page. A worst-case picture can read as fear selling. A plain sentence on what the buyer wanted to avoid is enough.

### Gaps
- Miller's book text was not available; the "customers buy internal solutions" line comes from summaries. The free PDF covers hero, guide, problem, plan, failure and success but does not include the later "BrandScript" wording.

## 6. Lisa Cron, Wired for Story: what the reader's brain looks for

### Takeaway
Cron argues that readers do not follow plot; they follow how events change the protagonist inside. A story is "how an unavoidable external problem forces the protagonist to change internally." Her brain-science framing is her interpretation of research, not her own tested work. Separate tested research (narrative transportation) supports a narrower point: readers who are absorbed in a story are more moved by it.

### Cited Findings
- "A story is not about an external change, it's about an internal change, within the protagonist's belief system." "A story is about how an unavoidable external problem forces the protagonist to change internally." "The narrative throughline is not the plot." Source: [Lisa Cron, Writer Unboxed, 2021-03-08](https://writerunboxed.com/2021/03/08/still-crazy-after-all-these-years/)
- What readers ask: "WHY? What on earth were they thinking?" And "we don't empathize with the plot, we empathize with the characters." Source: [Writer Unboxed, 2021](https://writerunboxed.com/2021/03/08/still-crazy-after-all-these-years/)
- On emotion: "you never have to tell us how they're feeling." On memory: "do not have your characters simply reminisce about the past." Backstory belongs in the moment, when the character makes a choice. Source: [Writer Unboxed, 2021](https://writerunboxed.com/2021/03/08/still-crazy-after-all-these-years/)
- Cron's short definition in a course: a story is about how what happens affects someone in pursuit of a "deceptively difficult goal" and how that person changes internally. Source: [CreativeLive lesson, "What is a story"](https://site.creativelive.com/class/fast-class-wired-for-story-how-to-become-a-story-genius-lisa-cron/lessons/what-is-a-story)
- Scene-level stakes question, as quoted by a reader: ask what the protagonist wants from the scene, then ask "What is at stake here?" Source: [Goodreads reader notes on Wired for Story](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/15747464-wired-for-story/20716860-j-d)
- Tested evidence: Green and Brock (2000), four experiments, found that readers more "transported" into a narrative held more story-consistent beliefs and rated the protagonist more favorably. Labelling a story fact or fiction did not change transportation; telling readers to watch surface features (such as grammar) lowered it. Source: [Green and Brock, 2000, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 79(5), full text](https://gwern.net/docs/www/pdfs.semanticscholar.org/39691cee32e52fbfc33f1a0316aa0d008cb699a4.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a retiree story, the internal change is the point: from "we can't leave the grandkids' town" to "we see them more now", or from "a smaller house feels like giving up" to "we have room for what we use". The house is the external problem; the change in belief is the story.
- Cron's "never tell us how they're feeling" gives a concrete edit rule: cut sentences like "They were thrilled." Show the act that proves it.
- Green and Brock's finding that surface focus lowers transportation supports keeping numbers, links and jargon out of the story's sentences.

### Gaps
- I did not reach Cron's book text. Her claim that fMRI shows the brain "searches first for the protagonist" appears in her own writing; I did not trace it to a specific study.
- A 2012 meta-analysis by Van Laer and others is said in one search summary to link transportation to characters the audience can identify with. I did not read it or a page that confirms it.

## 7. Flash fiction and micro-memoir craft: compression, starting in scene, one turn, a resonant ending, the telling detail

### Takeaway
Flash writers agree on compression (every sentence does more than one job), one image or moment, and an ending where something "rises that resonates" rather than a stated lesson. Brevity's editor treats the form as close to poetry. This is practitioner opinion.

### Cited Findings
- Dinty W. Moore, editor of Brevity: "Compression ... Every sentence has to do more than one thing." (From the free preview of an interview whose full text is paywalled.) Source: [Lit Mag News, Becky Tuch, 2025-01-07](https://litmagnews.substack.com/p/well-make-it-short-nonfiction-a-chat)
- Moore: "I think of flash nonfiction in metaphoric terms: like the moment you see a figure illuminated across the street during a brief middle-of-the-night flash of lightning. Though it is a prose form, it shares much with poetry: the concision of language and often the importance of a single image." Source: [Talking Writing, "Talking Flash Nonfiction with Brevity", 2020-11-16](https://mail.talkingwriting.com/talking-flash-nonfiction-brevity)
- Lee Martin, on teaching the brief essay in 25 words: "Pay close attention to the way compression creates the turn at the end of an essay, the one where something rises that resonates." He also says the flash essay "required a poet's attention to image and language while also accommodating the fiction writer's need to tell a good story." Source: [Talking Writing, 2020](https://mail.talkingwriting.com/talking-flash-nonfiction-brevity)
- Sue William Silverman, same roundtable: "Make the title 'work' like that of a poem. No prescriptive titles." Source: [Talking Writing, 2020](https://mail.talkingwriting.com/talking-flash-nonfiction-brevity)
- Brevity takes nonfiction of 750 words or fewer and asks for vivid detail, strong voice and no wasted words. Source: [Brevity submission page](https://brevity.submittable.com/submit/723/brief-nonfiction-750-words-or-less)
- Moore once assumed a short piece had to be one scene over a short span of time, and later decided that limit did not hold (the anthology includes lists and a piece told in footnotes). Source: [Escape Into Life review of The Best of Brevity](https://escapeintolife.com/book-reviews/briefly)
- A flash fiction workshop guide: a piece that tells the reader everything it means leaves no room for the reader's own feeling; everyday subjects work, but often fail when "the writer mistakes observation for insight." (Commercial workshop blog, lower authority.) Source: [Writing Workshops, flash fiction craft guide](https://writingworkshops.com/blogs/news/flash-fiction-masters-craft-guide)

### Inferences
- "Every sentence has to do more than one thing" is the working rule at 5 to 10 sentences. A sentence that sets the scene should also show the problem; a sentence that gives the detail should also show the feeling.
- Martin's "turn at the end" is the same slot as Dicks's five-second moment and Hall's explosion, moved late. In a very short story the turn often is the last or second-to-last sentence.
- The flash writers' preference for an unstated meaning supports ending on an image or action, then stating the reader's takeaway in the next paragraph outside the story (the nut graf pattern from section 1).

### Gaps
- I did not reach Tom Hazuka's or the Sudden Fiction editors' notes on craft. The anthologies' afterwords are in print only.
- Brevity's craft essay archive (now folded into the Brevity Blog) has a tag index for "Beginnings/Endings" and "Sensory Detail", but I did not find a single essay there on compression and the telling detail to quote.
- Moore's full Lit Mag News interview is paywalled; only the one compression line was visible.

## 8. Failures to avoid at short length, with before-and-after examples from the sources

### Takeaway
The sources name the same failures again and again: summary instead of a scene; numbers and abstraction in place of a person; a generic character; no stakes; a chain of "and then"; a moral spelled out; the company as the hero. Each has a source-backed fix. Only "too many numbers" has tested evidence behind it.

### Cited Findings
**Summary instead of scene**
- Wenzl: "When you write about things in the abstract, it's very difficult for readers to get engaged with it." Hertzel: scenes "should unfold moment by moment." Source: [Nieman Storyboard, 2013](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=1093)
- Hall: bring the audience to a specific time and place. Source: [Social Media Examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/creating-stories-that-stick-how-marketers-can-win-with-story-kindra-hall/). Dicks: start in motion, skip background. Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)

**Too many numbers (tested)**
- Adding statistics to one named person's story lowered giving. Small: "It's easy to override people's feelings by giving them statistical information." Source: [Knowledge at Wharton, 2007](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/understanding-emotional-appeals-how-to-get-chartiable-donations/); [Stanford GSB, 2007](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/increase-charitable-donations-appeal-heart-not-head)

**Generic characters**
- Hall: an identifiable person, not "the company"; the product is not a character. Source: [Social Media Examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/creating-stories-that-stick-how-marketers-can-win-with-story-kindra-hall/); [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=101525). Pixar rule 13: passive characters without opinions lose the audience. Source: [RedShark News](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling)

**No stakes**
- Pixar rule 16: say what is lost if the character fails. Source: [RedShark News](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling). Miller: "If we don't know what failure might befall the hero, audiences zone out and lose interest." Source: [StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)

**A list of events ("and then")**
- Stone: "this happened, and then this happened, and then this happens -- that's not a movie." Source: [Speakola transcript, 2014](https://speakola.com/arts/matt-stone-trey-parker-nyu-writing-class-2014)

**A moral stated outright, or feelings named**
- Cron: "you never have to tell us how they're feeling." Source: [Writer Unboxed, 2021](https://writerunboxed.com/2021/03/08/still-crazy-after-all-these-years/). Allen: narrative works "without summing up the story and telling readers what it's about." Source: [Nieman Storyboard, 2013](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=1093). Martin: the turn at the end is "the one where something rises that resonates." Source: [Talking Writing, 2020](https://mail.talkingwriting.com/talking-flash-nonfiction-brevity)

**The sales pitch ending, or the company as hero**
- Miller: "Your brand is not the hero of the story. Your customer is." Source: [StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)

**Other short-length failures named in the sources**
- A character who is "never seen or heard from again." Source: [Poynter, 2003](https://poynter.org/?p=11371)
- Announcing "the craziest story", which promises more than the story delivers. Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)
- A coincidence that solves the problem. Source: [RedShark News, rule 19](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling)
- Adding events that did not happen. Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)

**Before-and-after pairs the sources themselves give**
- Statistics versus one person: appeal B lists food shortages affecting millions across several African countries; appeal A tells of Rokia, a seven-year-old girl in Mali, and what a gift will do for her. A raised more; A plus B raised less than A. Source: [Knowledge at Wharton, 2007](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/understanding-emotional-appeals-how-to-get-chartiable-donations/); [Stanford GSB, 2007](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/increase-charitable-donations-appeal-heart-not-head)
- Abstract opening versus character: Wenzl replaced "an anecdotal lede followed by summary news nut graf" with a story wrapped "around these central characters," so readers wanted "to know what happened next to the girls." Source: [Nieman Storyboard, 2013](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=1093)
- Anecdote, then meaning: the water-gun victims' scene, then "Berry and Lee are victims of a new urban weapon in South St. Petersburg." Source: [Poynter, 2003](https://poynter.org/?p=11371)
- Opposite beginning: a story that ends "my mother was right" opens with the teller sure she was giving bad advice. Source: [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)

### Inferences
**A checklist for a 5 to 10 sentence story on the blog, built from the sources above**
1. Write the last sentence first: the small moment of change (Dicks, Coats rule 7).
2. Make sentence one its opposite, in the buyer's old life, in one place at one time (Dicks, Hall, Adams' "every day").
3. Name one person or couple with one opinion or want (Hall, Coats rule 13). Real names only from the story bank; otherwise the STANDARD T2 "Example:" label applies.
4. Put the problem in sentence one or two (Dicks's Elephant; Miller's external problem).
5. Link sentences with "but" and "therefore", never only "and then" (Parker and Stone).
6. One concrete detail the reader can picture; no model names, codes or road numbers (Hall, Heath, repo P4).
7. No numbers inside the story; at most one round number (Small et al.; repo P2).
8. The company appears at most once, as a guide doing a recorded task, in plain present tense (Miller; repo T2, C7).
9. End on an action or image, not a stated lesson or feeling (Cron, Allen, Martin).
10. Put the lesson for the reader in the next paragraph, outside the story (Scanlan's nut graf). If the page goes on, bring the person back once (Scanlan).

**Illustration of the technique only.** This is not a real story and must not be published. Any published story must come from `stories/stories.json` or carry the "Example:" label under STANDARD T2.
- Before (summary, numbers, company as hero): "Many retirees want single-level living. Our team helped a couple from Ohio find a ranch home with 1,600 square feet, 2 bedrooms and a 2-car garage. They were thrilled with the result and love their new community."
- After (scene, one detail, but/therefore, buyer as hero): "Example: Every night in Ohio, Carol carried the laundry basket down fourteen basement stairs. Her knee hurt on every trip back up. But the houses they toured on their first trip all had a second floor. So they asked to see only homes where the washer sat next to the kitchen. On the fourth one, Carol set her purse on the dryer and did not go upstairs, because there was no upstairs." The meaning for the reader ("a one-story plan matters more than the square footage") goes in the next paragraph.
- The "after" version has one number ("fourteen"), which is a picturable detail, not a statistic. Whether even that number should stay is a judgment for the reviewer under repo rule P2.

**Evidence strength, in one place**
- Tested: identifiable person beats statistics, and statistics can lower the effect of a story (Small et al. 2007, with a 2024 replication I did not read); people absorbed in a story hold more story-consistent beliefs (Green and Brock 2000); experts overestimate how clear they are (Newton 1990, as told by the Heaths).
- Expert opinion: everything about structure (spine, but/therefore, normal/explosion/new normal, five-second moment, three-level problem, nut graf, compression, endings).

### Gaps
- None of the sources tests the best length for a marketing anecdote. "5 to 10 sentences" is the brief's constraint, not a finding. Dicks's "the shorter story wins" is opinion.
- I found no study testing a stated moral against an unstated one, or a sales-pitch ending against a plain one, in marketing stories. The advice against both is practitioner consensus.
- No source spoke directly to stories for retirees or older readers. The transfer to this audience is my inference.
