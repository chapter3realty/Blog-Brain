# The science of why stories persuade and are remembered, graded by evidence strength

Grade key used on every finding:
- **[A]** Meta-analysis, or a large pre-registered replication. Treat as reliable for direction; read the effect size.
- **[B]** A single study, or a few studies from one lab. Plausible, not settled.
- **[C]** Popular claim with a weak source or none. Do not repeat.
- **[X]** Contested: a failed replication or a bias-corrected reanalysis cuts against it.

Most numbers below were read from the primary abstract or full text. Where a number came only from a secondary source, the line says so.

## 1. Narrative transportation theory: what it predicts, effect sizes, and what raises transportation

### Takeaway
Transportation (being absorbed in a story: attention, imagery, emotion) is one of the best-supported ideas in this field. Readers who are more absorbed hold more story-consistent attitudes (medium correlation), but the causal effect of getting a story instead of no story is small (r of about .06 to .23). Identifiable characters, a plot the reader can picture, and lifelikeness each raise transportation by a small amount.

### Cited Findings
- **[B, later confirmed by [A]]** Green and Brock (2000) defined transportation as absorption into a story involving imagery, affect and attentional focus. They built the transportation scale. In four experiments (N = 97, 69, 274, 258), more transported readers held more story-consistent beliefs and rated the protagonist more favorably. Highly transported readers found fewer "false notes" in the story. ([Green and Brock 2000, JPSP, doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701); abstract details via [gwern mirror of the paper](https://gwern.net/docs/www/pdfs.semanticscholar.org/39691cee32e52fbfc33f1a0316aa0d008cb699a4.pdf))
- **[B]** Labeling the same story as fact or fiction usually did not change transportation (Green and Brock 2000; Strange and Leung 1999; Chlebuch et al. 2020). But a fact label prompted more critical scrutiny of weak arguments than a fiction label, at least for readers low in need for cognition (Green et al. 2006). ([Green and Appel 2024 review, preprint pp. 30-31](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))
- **[A]** van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti and Wetzels (2014) meta-analyzed 132 effect sizes from 76 articles. Reliability-adjusted correlations with transportation ([accepted manuscript, Table 2](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/); [JCR doi](https://doi.org/10.1086/673383)):
  - Storyteller-controlled antecedents: identifiable character ρ = .20 (k = 16, N = 3,134); imaginable plot ρ = .29 (k = 28, N = 4,117); verisimilitude (lifelikeness) ρ = .27 (k = 8, N = 1,524).
  - Reader antecedents: familiarity ρ = .21; attention ρ = .29; transportability (trait) ρ = .30; education ρ = .10; **age ρ = .00 (k = 34, N = 5,691)**.
  - Consequences: affective response ρ = .57; critical thoughts ρ = -.20; narrative thoughts ρ = .20; beliefs ρ = .26; attitudes ρ = .44; intentions ρ = .31.
  - The authors grade the antecedent effects as "small but not trivial" and the attitude and intention links as medium.
  - Caution: the consequence figures are correlations between measured transportation and outcomes. They are not the effect of adding a story. They are larger than the causal effects in the next two lines.
- **[A]** Braddock and Dillard (2016) meta-analyzed experiments comparing narrative exposure with no narrative or a non-narrative. Story-consistent beliefs r = .17 (k = 37, N = 7,376); attitudes r = .19 (k = 40, N = 7,132); intentions r = .17 (k = 28, N = 5,211); behaviors r = .23 (k = 5, N = 978). Fictionality moderator results were mixed. ([Communication Monographs, doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555); figures as reported in the abstract, via [SSRC MediaWell citation](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/?p=52168))
- **[A]** Shen, Sheer and Li (2015): 25 health studies (N = 9,330). Narratives had a small effect on persuasion (r = .063). Audio and video narratives worked; **print narratives showed no significant effect**. Prevention and detection narratives worked; cessation narratives did not. ([Penn State record of J. Advertising paper](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/impact-of-narratives-on-persuasion-in-health-communication-a-meta/); [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2015.1018467))
- **[A]** Oschatz and Marker (2020): 14 studies. A single narrative beat a non-narrative on attitudes and intentions right after exposure, and on attitudes, intentions and behaviors at delayed measurement. Narratives did not beat non-narratives on beliefs. Transportation mediated the immediate effects. ([J. Communication, doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqaa017); abstract via Semantic Scholar)
- **[A]** van Laer, Feiereisen and Visconti (2019): 138 effect sizes from 64 articles. The transportation effect was stronger when the story was in a commercial domain (not non-commercial), was user-generated (not professional), and was received by one person at a time. ([J. Business Research, doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2018.10.053))
- **[A, small]** Similarity between reader and character has a small but significant effect on identification. Psychological similarity has larger effects than objective (demographic) similarity (Huang et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2023 meta-analyses). Broad demographic matching does not reliably raise identification. ([Green and Appel 2024, p. 15](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))
- **[A, small]** First-person ("I") narratives produced more identification than third-person ones in a meta-analysis of 16 health studies, but no significant difference in transportation (Chen and Bell 2022). (Same source, pp. 15-16)
- **[B]** Narrativity raises transportation: a causal sequence of events, agents with goals, a conflict, and at least one turning point. Attention narrows at suspenseful points (Bezdek and Gerrig 2017). Plausibility matters more than factual accuracy for realism; story details that break expectations (such as modern technology in a story set in the past) lowered realism and engagement (Busselle and Vierrether 2022). (Same source, pp. 7, 17-18, 41-43)
- **[B]** Claims placed in a causal position in a story (they change what happens next) were more memorable and more persuasive than the same claims in non-causal positions, and were judged truer two weeks later (Dahlstrom 2010, 2012). ([Dahlstrom 2010, Communication Research, doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650210362683); summary in [Green and Appel 2024, pp. 43-44](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))
- **[B]** A "transitional" character (skeptical at first, then converted) persuaded more than a character who was positive from the start, mediated by self-referencing, empathy and similarity (Huang and Green, in press). ([Green and Appel 2024, p. 38](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))

### Inferences
- A writer can rely on this: a short story with one identifiable person, a plot the reader can picture, and lifelike detail will tend to absorb readers more than a story without them. The expected gain is small.
- Transportation did not vary with age in the van Laer data (ρ = .00). There is no evidence-based reason to write differently for older readers on transportation grounds.
- Stories move attitudes and intentions more than factual beliefs (Braddock and Dillard; Oschatz and Marker; Zebregs, below). For a blog: use the facts section to set beliefs (prices, rules, numbers), and the story to make the decision feel doable.
- The rule "put the key point where it causes the next event" (Dahlstrom) fits a 5 to 10 sentence story: the lesson should be the thing that changed what the buyer did.
- Shen et al. found print health narratives had no significant effect. Text-only stories on a blog should be expected to do modest work.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis or experiment on very short (5 to 10 sentence) text stories specifically. Most studies use longer texts, videos, or ads.
- I found no study of narrative persuasion in residential real estate or home buying.
- The Braddock and Dillard numbers came from an abstract mirror, not the full paper; the full text is paywalled.

## 2. Paul Zak's oxytocin and "neural" narrative research

### Takeaway
Zak's claims that stories release oxytocin and that this drives trust and giving rest mainly on small studies from his own lab, using plasma oxytocin assays and intranasal dosing that later critiques found unreliable or underpowered. The parent finding (oxytocin increases trust) failed a large pre-registered replication. What is still defensible is the behavioral part: stories with tension and an emotional arc hold attention and raise empathy. The hormone mechanism should not be cited.

### Cited Findings
- **What was claimed [B]:** In a 2015 Cerebrum essay, Zak reports that a ~100-second video of a father and his 2-year-old son Ben, who has terminal cancer, with "a classic dramatic arc," raised blood cortisol and oxytocin, while a "flat" zoo video did not and produced no reported empathy. He reports that 40 people given 40 IU oxytocin or placebo donated "56 percent more money"; that in 42 participants, donations were "261 percent higher" when both ACTH and oxytocin rose; and that a physiological model "predicts whether a participant would donate money with 82 percent accuracy" (no citation attached to that sentence). He also reports "zero correlation" between Super Bowl ad viewer ratings and his attention-emotion measure, with no citation. ([Zak 2015, Cerebrum, PMC4445577](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4445577/))
- **[B]** Barraza and Zak (2009): empathy while watching an emotional video clip was associated with a 47% rise in plasma oxytocin from baseline, stronger in women, and more empathy went with more generous ultimatum-game offers. Single study. ([Annals NYAS, doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-6632.2009.04504.x); abstract via Europe PMC, PMID 19580564)
- **[B]** Lin et al. (2013), Zak's lab: after 16 public service ads, participants given intranasal oxytocin donated to 57% more causes, donated 56% more money, and reported 17% more concern than placebo. In a second experiment, donations occurred when both ACTH and oxytocin rose. ([PLoS ONE, doi](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0056934))
- **[B]** Barraza et al. (2015), Zak's lab: heart and skin-conductance measures during the Ben story (including HF-HRV) predicted who donated, and heart activity tracked moment-to-moment concern. This study used peripheral physiology, not oxytocin. ([Biological Psychology, doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2015.01.008); PMID 25617658)
- **[X] The parent trust finding:** Kosfeld, Heinrichs, Zak et al. (2005) reported that intranasal oxytocin increases trust ([Nature, doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature03701)). Nave, Camerer and McCullough (2015) concluded "the cumulative evidence does not provide robust convergent evidence that human trust is reliably associated with OT (or caused by it)"; the simplest intranasal finding "has not replicated well"; plasma evidence is flawed by how oxytocin is measured in peripheral fluids; large genetic studies found no consistent links. ([Perspectives on Psychological Science, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26581735/); [author PDF](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~memccullough/Papers/NaveCamererMcCullough15.pdf))
- **[A] Failed registered replication:** Declerck, Boone, Pauwels, Vogt and Fehr (2020) ran a registered report with more than 95% power (677 men, Magdeburg and Antwerp). They found no effect of oxytocin on trust in the trust game. A post hoc subgroup hint needs confirmation. ([Nature Human Behaviour, doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0878-x); [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nathum/v4y2020i6d10.1038_s41562-020-0878-x.html); sample size from [U. Antwerp blog](https://blog.uantwerpen.be/business-economics/what-makes-us-trust-another-person-a-behavioural-study-on-the-role-of-oxytocin/))
- **[B]** Lane et al. (2015): two replications powered to detect the original large effect found no oxytocin effect on trust in the envelope task. ([PLoS ONE, PMC4569325](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4569325/))
- **Measurement critique:** McCullough, Churchland and Mendez (2013): commercial oxytocin assays "lack reliability when used on unextracted samples of human fluids," tag molecules other than oxytocin, and give estimates "wildly discrepant" with prior findings. An earlier study from the same group found unextracted plasma read more than 100-fold higher than extracted plasma, with minimal correlation between the two. ([Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews, doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2013.04.018); [author PDF](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~memccullough/Papers/CV%20Papers/PeripheralOxytocin_NBR_2013.pdf))
- **Power critique:** Walum, Waldman and Young (2016): "intranasal OT studies are generally underpowered and ... there is a high probability that most of the published intranasal OT findings do not represent true effects." ([Biological Psychiatry, doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2015.06.016); PMID 26210057)
- **Other critics [weak sourcing]:** Wikipedia lists Conlisk (2011, J. Economic Behavior and Organization) raising methodological questions about Zak's trust studies, Molly Crockett arguing oxytocin can increase gloating and in-group bias, and Ed Yong noting it can increase envy and schadenfreude. I only read Wikipedia's account of these. ([Wikipedia: Paul J. Zak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_J._Zak); [Conlisk doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2011.01.002))
- **[A, behavioral part only]** Separate from hormones, the van Laer meta-analysis links transportation to affective response (ρ = .57), and reviews link suspense to narrowed attention. ([van Laer 2014, Table 2](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/); [Green and Appel 2024](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))

### Inferences
- Do not write "stories release oxytocin," "the trust hormone," or "neuroscience proves" in any copy or internal rule. The mechanism is not established, and the trust claim it grew from failed a large replication.
- Defensible: a story with tension (a problem, a turn, a resolution) holds attention and evokes empathy better than a flat account. That rests on the transportation literature, not on Zak.
- Zak's specific percentages (56%, 261%, 82%) are single-lab, small-sample figures. Treat as [B] at best; the 82% figure has no citation in the essay.

### Gaps
- I found no independent replication of the Ben-video oxytocin result or of Lin et al. (2013).
- I could not confirm whether Zak's narrative studies extracted plasma before assay, which is the core of the McCullough critique.
- I found no peer-reviewed independent test of Zak's commercial "Immersion" measure.

## 3. The identifiable victim effect: size and limits

### Takeaway
The effect that one named, pictured person draws more help than statistics about many is small at best (r = .05 in the 2016 meta-analysis) and may be near zero after correcting for publication bias. A 2024 pre-registered replication of the classic study found no effect. It applies to charity appeals and gives weak guidance for real estate stories.

### Cited Findings
- **[B, now X]** Small, Loewenstein and Slovic (2007): people leaving a psychology experiment could give up to $5 of their earnings to Save the Children. A story about one girl (Rokia) drew more than statistics about millions in need. Adding statistics to Rokia's story lowered giving. Teaching people about the effect made them give less to the identifiable victim without giving more to statistical victims. "Insight, in this situation, seems to breed callousness." ([Stanford GSB summary](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/increase-charitable-donations-appeal-heart-not-head); [OBHDP doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2006.01.005))
- **[A, small]** Lee and Feeley (2016): random-effects meta-analysis of 41 studies. "An overall significant yet modest IVE (r = .05)." Most reliable with a single victim (identified or not), or with a photographed child in poverty, low responsibility for the need, and a request for money. ([SKKU record of Social Influence paper](https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/the-identifiable-victim-effect-a-meta-analytic-review/); [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/15534510.2016.1216891))
- **[X]** Maier, Wong and Feldman (2024): a robust Bayesian reanalysis of Lee and Feeley's data "suggested publication bias in the literature." Their pre-registered replication of Small et al. Studies 1 and 3 (hypothetical donations) "found no empirical support for the identifiable victim effect (ηp² = .000, 95% CI [.000, .003])" and no support for the debiasing result. The authors note real-money donations were not used. ([Collabra: Psychology, doi](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.90203))
- **[X, secondhand]** A secondary quote of the reanalysis reports "strong evidence for the absence of an identified victim effect (BF01 = 14.93), with a model-averaged mean effect size estimate of r = 0.002." I could not read this sentence in the primary text. ([PCI RR reviewer document citing it](https://rr.peercommunityin.org/download/t_reviews.review_pdf.bd797982a0752635.323032342d30352047696c61642046656c646d616e20726576696577205043492d5252206f6666656e646572206964656e746966696162696c6974792e646f6378.docx))

### Inferences
- Do not justify the story format with the identifiable victim effect. Justify it with transportation and memory (sections 1 and 5), which hold up better.
- The part that is consistent across sources is narrow: one person reads as more real than a group. For a 5 to 10 sentence story, one buyer or one couple is a safe default.
- Small et al. found that stacking statistics onto a single story reduced giving. That result is now contested, so it is not a reason to strip numbers from a page. It is a mild reason to keep the story and the numbers in separate paragraphs.

### Gaps
- I could not verify per-condition dollar amounts for Small et al. (2007). A figure of $2.38 for the Rokia group appears only in a reader's note on *Made to Stick* ([Goodreads note](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/36692830-made-to-stick/8083056-britney-faustine/806bf376-f5d3-49f5-aeb7-f6dfb267bca7)). Treat it as unverified.
- No study found on identifiable-person effects in non-charity buying decisions.

## 4. Peak-end rule and serial position: what they imply for ending a short story

### Takeaway
When people look back on an experience, the most intense moment and the final moment carry heavy weight, and length barely matters (meta-analytic r = .58). People judging another person's life also weight its ending heavily. The evidence comes from experiences and life-story judgments, not from reading short texts. "End on a clear, resolved final beat" is a reasonable extrapolation, not a tested rule for blog stories.

### Cited Findings
- **[B]** Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier (1993), "When more pain is preferred to less: adding a better end": participants who held a hand in painful cold water preferred to repeat a longer trial whose last part was slightly less painful over a shorter trial without that milder end. ([Psychological Science, doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x); full text not accessed. Design details such as 14°C for 60 s plus 30 s rising to 15°C come only from secondary summaries, e.g. [Brainscape notes](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/studies-kahneman-et-al-1993-14967126/packs/21787131))
- **[B, large RCT]** Redelmeier, Katz and Kahneman (2003): 682 colonoscopy patients randomized. Those given a short added interval with a less painful end rated the final moments as less painful (1.7 vs 2.5 on a 10-point scale), the whole procedure as less unpleasant (4.4 vs 4.9), ranked it less aversive (4.1 vs 4.6), and were slightly more likely to return for a repeat colonoscopy (odds ratio 1.41, p = .038; median follow-up 5.3 years). ([Pain, doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0304-3959(03)00003-4); PMID 12855328)
- **[A]** Alaybek, Dalal et al. (2022): 174 effect sizes. The peak-end effect on retrospective evaluations was "large (r = 0.581, 95% CI 0.487 to 0.661)," robust across boundary conditions, "comparable to the effect of the overall average (mean) score," stronger than the first (beginning) and lowest moments, and stronger than duration, whose effect "was essentially nil." ([OBHDP, doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149); [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html))
- **[B, three studies, one lab]** Diener, Wirtz and Oishi (2001), the "James Dean effect": when judging another person's whole life, participants weighted the end heavily and neglected duration. A wonderful life that ended abruptly was rated better than the same life plus mildly pleasant extra years. A terrible life with moderately bad extra years at the end was rated better than one ending abruptly without them. The same years placed in the middle of a life had weaker effects than at the end. ([Psychological Science, doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00321); PMID 11340920)
- **[A, classic]** Serial position: free recall of word lists shows better memory for the first items (primacy) and the last items (recency). ([Murdock 1962, J. Experimental Psychology, doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0045106); full text not accessed)
- **[B]** Within stories, attention peaks at the most suspenseful points (Bezdek and Gerrig 2017), and Zak reports peak attention at the climax (single lab, uncited). ([Green and Appel 2024, p. 7](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf); [Zak 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4445577/))

### Inferences
- For a 5 to 10 sentence story: give it one clear high point (the moment the problem bit, or the moment it was solved) and end on the outcome, stated concretely. Do not trail off into a general moral or a sales line; by the peak-end and recency evidence, the last sentence carries extra weight.
- A mildly pleasant extra sentence after a strong ending may dilute it (James Dean effect). Stop when the story is resolved.
- The meta-analysis found the peak-end score predicts about as well as the overall average. A good ending does not rescue a weak middle.
- These findings are about remembered experiences and judged lives. Applying them to how a reader remembers a short text is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- I found no experiment testing peak-end or ending effects on memory or persuasion for short written stories.
- I did not retrieve the abstract of Haugtvedt and Wegener (1994), "Message order effects in persuasion: an attitude strength perspective" ([JCR, doi](https://doi.org/10.1086/209393)), so I cannot report when primacy or recency wins in persuasion.

## 5. Memory: narrative versus expository text, and the "22 times more memorable" claim

### Takeaway
A 2021 meta-analysis of over 33,000 participants found stories are recalled better than expository text by about 0.7 of a standard deviation and understood better by about 0.5. That is a solid, moderate advantage. The claim that stories are "22 times more memorable than facts" has no primary source; it is attributed to Jerome Bruner without a citation and appears in a Jennifer Aaker talk without a study.

### Cited Findings
- **[A]** Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta (2021): 37 articles, 78 samples, 33,078 participants, 150 effect sizes. Overall narrative advantage g = .55 (95% CI .31 to .79). Memory g = .72 (95% CI .43 to 1.01, k = 57). Comprehension g = .48 (95% CI .21 to .75, k = 93). No moderator was significant: adults g = .44 vs non-adults g = .67; listening vs reading, content-controlled vs not, and immediate vs delayed testing all differed little. Robust to leave-one-out analyses. ([Psychonomic Bulletin and Review, PMC8219577](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/))
- **Limits of Mar et al. [A]:** heterogeneity was very high (I² = 98%); Egger's test showed some publication bias for memory (p = .02) but not comprehension; only 28% of effect sizes came from studies that controlled content; only four tested delayed recall. The authors caution against forcing all information into narrative form. Earlier single studies had found an advantage for essays or no difference. (Same source)
- **[B, secondhand]** Graesser, Hoffman and Clark (1980) is widely cited as finding narratives were read about twice as fast and remembered about twice as well as expository texts. I found this only in secondary accounts. ([J. Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(80)90132-2))
- **[B]** Bower and Clark (1969): people who made up their own story linking word lists recalled far more words than those who rehearsed them. A secondary account reports 93% recall in the story group. This is a memory technique where the learner writes the story, not a test of reading someone else's story. ([Psychonomic Science, doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03332778); figure via [Patience-Davies, LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/just-where-did-claim-stories-22-times-more-memorable-patience-davies-ud7le/))
- **[B]** Transportation into a health film predicted knowledge gain about the facts in it (Murphy et al. 2011, 2013). Mar et al. propose stories help memory through familiar structure (goal, conflict, resolution), social content, prior knowledge, and emotion. ([Green and Appel 2024, pp. 43, 46](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))
- **[C] "22 times more memorable":**
  - Stanford's Women's Leadership site quotes Jennifer Aaker: "Stories are remembered up to 22 times more than facts alone." The page cites no study for the number. ([Stanford Women's Leadership site](https://womensleadership.stanford.edu/resources/voice-influence/harnessing-power-stories))
  - Many pages credit Jerome Bruner with no page reference, e.g. "Jerome Bruner suggests that stories are about 22 times more memorable than facts alone." ([Anecdote](https://www.anecdote.com/?p=7151))
  - A writer who read Bruner's *Actual Minds, Possible Worlds* found "nothing whatsoever about stories being more memorable than facts," could not find the figure in Aaker's books, and found it only in one Aaker video stated "with no explanation of how the number was determined." ([Patience-Davies, LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/just-where-did-claim-stories-22-times-more-memorable-patience-davies-ud7le/))
  - A second check reached the same result: "So, does storytelling improve learning? Probably. 22 times? Unlikely." ([Intela Learning blog](https://intelalearning.wordpress.com/2020/07/23/storytelling-works-doesnt-it/))
- **[C]** The often-quoted "63% remembered the stories, 5% remembered a statistic" comes from a classroom exercise described in Chip and Dan Heath's *Made to Stick*, not a controlled study. ([Patience-Davies, LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/just-where-did-claim-stories-22-times-more-memorable-patience-davies-ud7le/))

### Inferences
- A defensible plain-language version: "In controlled studies, people remember stories better than plain explanations." Do not attach a multiplier.
- By standard conversion (Cohen's U3, my calculation, not from the paper), g = .72 means the typical story reader recalls more than about 76% of expository readers. That is a real but ordinary advantage, not 22-fold.
- The Mar et al. comparisons are whole texts. They do not show that adding a 5 to 10 sentence story to a factual article raises recall of the article's facts.

### Gaps
- I could not access the Graesser et al. (1980) full text to confirm "twice as fast, twice as well."
- No primary source for "22 times" was found in Bruner, Aaker, or elsewhere. I cannot rule out an unpublished source, but none is cited by anyone repeating it.

## 6. Concreteness, imagery (dual coding), and specific sensory detail

### Takeaway
Concrete, easy-to-picture text is rated more understandable and interesting and is recalled better; this is well supported in educational psychology. Imagery helps persuasion when it is easy to form and backfires when it is hard. The claim that concrete wording makes statements seem truer failed a pre-registered replication.

### Cited Findings
- **Theory:** Paivio's dual coding theory holds that concrete language can be stored both as words and as mental images, which aids memory; Clark and Paivio (1991) applied it to education. ([Paivio 1991, Canadian J. Psychology, doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0084295); [Clark and Paivio 1991, Educational Psychology Review, doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01320076); full texts not accessed)
- **[B, several experiments, one lab]** Sadoski, Goetz and Rodriguez (2000), four experiments across four text types: concreteness (ease of imagery) was "the variable overwhelmingly most related to comprehensibility and recall." A later review reports text interest correlated with concreteness at .85. Concrete titles aided recall of stories and narratives but not of persuasive or expository texts. ([J. Educational Psychology, doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.85); details via [Trames 2010 review](https://kirj.ee/public/trames_pdf/2010/issue_1/trames-2010-1-54-70.pdf) and search summaries; see also [Sadoski, Goetz and Fritz 1993, doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.85.2.291))
- **[C]** A communications blog says students "remembered the concrete text 70% better." I could not find that figure in a primary source. ([Wylie Communications](https://www.wyliecomm.com/concrete-images-in-writing/))
- **[A]** In the van Laer meta-analysis, an "imaginable plot" had the largest storyteller-controlled link to transportation (ρ = .29). ([van Laer 2014, Table 2](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/))
- **[B]** Readers trained to form vivid images across senses (sight, sound, smell, touch) were more transported and later more prosocial (Johnson, Cushman et al. 2013). Story-relevant pictures increased imagery fluency and persuasion (Cohen et al. 2020). The review notes "relatively little narrative persuasion research has directly examined the role of imagery." ([Green and Appel 2024, p. 37](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))
- **[B]** Petrova and Cialdini (2005), three studies: when imagery is hard to form, "imagine" appeals were ineffective and sometimes harmful, for people low in imagery ability or when the product was not presented vividly. ([JCR, doi](https://doi.org/10.1086/497556); [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i3p442-452.html))
- **[X]** Hansen and Wänke (2010) found concretely worded trivia statements were judged more likely true ([PSPB, doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167210386238)). A pre-registered replication found much smaller, non-significant effects (dz = 0.08, 95% CI -0.03 to 0.18; and dz = 0.11, 95% CI -0.01 to 0.22) and concluded the pattern "is inconsistent with that of the original study." ([Henderson, Vallée-Tourangeau and Simons 2019, Collabra, doi](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.192))

### Inferences
- Concrete detail earns its place by making the scene easy to picture: a named street type, a sound, a room, a time of day. Easy-to-picture beats vivid-but-vague.
- Do not use concrete wording as a trick to make a claim seem true. That effect did not replicate, and in this repo facts must come from the ledger anyway.
- An "imagine yourself" sentence only helps if the scene is easy to form. A reader who has never seen the place may find it hard; give one or two concrete anchors first.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis of the concreteness effect for prose recall; the strongest prose evidence is one research group (Sadoski and Goetz).
- I found no experiment isolating sensory detail (smell, sound, touch) in short persuasive stories.

## 7. Mental simulation and self-referencing (Escalas)

### Takeaway
Ads that lead people to imagine themselves using a product increased transportation and liking (Escalas 2004). When people are absorbed in a self-related story, weak arguments hurt persuasion less (Escalas 2007), though a later study found argument strength still matters. Relating material to oneself improves memory (meta-analysis). Psychological similarity to a character helps a little; demographic matching helps less.

### Cited Findings
- **[B]** Escalas (2004), "Imagine yourself in the product": ads that encouraged people to imagine using a pair of running shoes created transportation, which led to more positive ad attitudes and brand evaluations. ([J. Advertising, doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2004.10639163); finding as summarized in [Green and Appel 2024, p. 48](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))
- **[B]** Escalas (2007), two experiments: narrative self-referencing persuades through transportation, and when ad viewers are transported, weak ad arguments do not significantly reduce persuasion. Analytical self-referencing works through elaboration, so argument strength matters. Ad skepticism moderates the narrative transportation effect. ([JCR, doi](https://doi.org/10.1086/510216); [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v33y2007i4p421-429.html))
- **[B, conflicts with Escalas 2007]** Schreiner, Appel, Isberner and Richter found stories with strong arguments were more persuasive than stories with weak ones, and the gap was largest for deeply transported readers. ([preprint](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/user_upload/Publikationen/Schreiner_Appel_Isberner_Richter_PrePrint.pdf), finding as reported in search summary; full text not read)
- **[A]** Symons and Johnson (1997): self-referent encoding produced better memory than semantic or other-referent encoding. The advantage was smaller against other-reference and when comparison tasks also encouraged organization and elaboration. ([Psychological Bulletin, doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.121.3.371); PMID 9136641). Note: this is memory for words and traits, not for stories.
- **[A, small]** Similarity raises identification a little; psychological similarity more than demographic similarity. ([Green and Appel 2024, p. 15](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))
- **[B]** Clearly linking the product to the story (showing it in use, or showing it solving the problem) increased transportation and ad effectiveness (Glaser and Reisinger 2022). (Same source, p. 48)
- **[B]** In anti-smoking narratives, transportation raised quit intentions through both cognitive and experiential routes, including self-referencing (Dunlop et al. 2010). (Same source, p. 47)

### Inferences
- For retiree buyers, match the reader's situation and worry (selling a long-held house, a fixed budget, a move away from family), not just their age bracket. That is the "psychological similarity" finding.
- A story that shows the buyer facing the same choice the reader faces, then making it, lets the reader rehearse the decision. Keep the service's role in the story concrete (what was done), per Glaser and Reisinger.
- Do not rely on absorption to carry a weak point (Schreiner et al.). The story should make a true, useful point.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full text of Escalas 2004 or 2007, so sample sizes and effect sizes are not reported here.
- I found no study testing second-person ("you") prompts inside short written stories.

## 8. Counter-evidence: when stories backfire or persuade less

### Takeaway
Stories do not always win. Statistics beat stories for changing beliefs and attitudes in one meta-analysis; print health stories showed no significant effect in another. When readers infer manipulative intent, the narrative advantage can vanish, though a meta-analysis found narratives still cause less resistance on average. Implausible details, hard-to-imagine scenes, and stacking a lesson onto the story can all reduce the effect.

### Cited Findings
- **[A]** Zebregs, van den Putte, Neijens and de Graaf (2015): "Statistical evidence was found to have a stronger influence than narrative evidence on beliefs and attitude, whereas narrative evidence had a stronger influence on intention." ([Health Communication, doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/10410236.2013.842528); PMID 24836931)
- **[A, small, old]** Allen and Preiss (1997), an early small meta-analysis, found statistical messages more persuasive than narratives. A systematic review of vaccination messages found narratives beat statistics about half the time, with very small effects (Lazić and Žeželj 2021). ([Allen and Preiss doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/08824099709388654); both summarized in [Green and Appel 2024, p. 45](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))
- **[A]** Shen, Sheer and Li (2015): print narratives showed no significant effect; cessation narratives showed none. ([Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/impact-of-narratives-on-persuasion-in-health-communication-a-meta/))
- **[B]** Wentzel, Tomczak and Herrmann (2010): when manipulative intent was not salient, narrative ads were rated better than expository ads. When it was made salient (by adding rhetorical questions), consumers switched to analytical processing and "the relative advantage of narrative ads over expository ads disappears," mediated by inferences of manipulative intent. ([Psychology and Marketing, doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.20341); [St. Gallen record](https://alexandria.unisg.ch/61214); manipulation detail from a [Radboud thesis](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/bitstreams/5f2df299-11ec-460d-ab69-599cf1206ad8/download))
- **[A, conflicts in part with Wentzel]** Ratcliff and Sun (2020): narratives produced less resistance than non-narratives (d = -.213, 15 effect sizes, 9 experiments), and engagement correlated negatively with resistance (r = -.131, 25 studies), moderated by genre, length, medium and character unit. Green and Appel report this held "even for cases in which the persuasive intent of a narrative was obvious." ([Human Communication Research, doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/hcr/hqz017); [Green and Appel 2024, p. 39](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf))
- **[B]** Ad skepticism moderates narrative transportation (Escalas 2007). ([IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v33y2007i4p421-429.html))
- **[B]** Transported readers may still counterargue against a character within the story, for example one who promotes a harmful position (Moyer-Gusé and Nabi 2011). Theory on why entertainment narratives reduce resistance: Moyer-Gusé (2008). ([Green and Appel 2024, pp. 39-40](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf); [Moyer-Gusé 2008, doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2885.2008.00328.x))
- **[B]** Details that break the reader's expectations of realism (anachronisms) reduced perceived realism and engagement (Busselle and Vierrether 2022; Green and Garst 2008). (Same source, pp. 42-43)
- **[B]** A fact label prompted more scrutiny of weak arguments than a fiction label (Green et al. 2006). (Same source, p. 30)
- **[B]** Imagery appeals backfire when imagery is hard to form (Petrova and Cialdini 2005). ([IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i3p442-452.html))
- **[B]** Identifiable characters who model a behavior can increase readers' intention to do it, even when the writer did not intend that (characters who smoke, cited in van Laer 2014 from earlier work). ([van Laer 2014 accepted manuscript](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/))
- **[X]** Adding statistics to an identifiable-victim story reduced giving in Small et al. (2007), but the base effect did not replicate in 2024. ([Maier et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.90203))
- **[A, surprising direction]** Transportation effects were stronger for commercial stories than non-commercial ones in a 2019 meta-analysis, so commercial context alone does not kill the effect. ([van Laer et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2018.10.053))

### Inferences
- On a realtor's blog, persuasive intent is always visible. The evidence is mixed on whether that hurts. The safe reading: keep the sales line and rhetorical questions out of the story itself; put the call to action after it. Wentzel's manipulation of intent was rhetorical questions, which is a specific thing to avoid inside a story.
- Do not replace numbers with stories. Use numbers to set beliefs and the story to support the decision (Zebregs).
- Because a "true story" label invites scrutiny, every detail must be accurate and plausible. One wrong or odd detail can lower realism and engagement. This matches this repo's rule to use only real stories from the story bank and add no detail.
- A story that shows a buyer making a mistake vividly may model the mistake. Show the turn and the better choice, the "transitional character" pattern from section 1.

### Gaps
- I found no study of narrative backfire in real estate or other high-cost, infrequent purchases.
- I found no direct test of reactance to stories placed inside commercial blog posts.
- The 2019 commercial-domain result and the 2010 manipulative-intent result were not reconciled in any source I read.
