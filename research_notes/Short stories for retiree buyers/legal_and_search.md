# Stories and examples in real estate marketing: legal limits (US and South Carolina) and search value

Researched 2026-10-10. Primary sources were read directly where possible: eCFR text pulled through the eCFR API on 2026-10-10, the Federal Register preamble for 16 CFR 465, the SC Code on scstatehouse.gov, HUD withdrawal notices in the Federal Register, and the Google Search Rater Guidelines PDF (September 11, 2025). Each line ends with its source link. Secondary sources are marked "secondary".

## 1. FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255 (revised 2023)

### Takeaway
A story or quote counts as an endorsement when readers are likely to believe it shows the experience of someone other than the advertiser. An "obviously fictional dramatization" is not an endorsement. A real customer story about a key result is read as typical, unless the page states the generally expected result. "Results not typical" disclaimers do not work.

### Cited Findings
- The current Guides were published at 88 FR 48102 on July 26, 2023. Source: [eCFR 16 CFR Part 255](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255)
- The Guides are FTC interpretations of Section 5 of the FTC Act. They are not binding rules. "Practices inconsistent with these Guides may result in corrective action by the Commission under section 5." Source: [16 CFR 255.0(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)
- Definition: an endorsement is "any advertising, marketing, or promotional message for a product that consumers are likely to believe reflects the opinions, beliefs, findings, or experiences of a party other than the sponsoring advertiser." "Product" includes "any product, service, brand, company, or industry", so a brokerage's services are covered. Source: [16 CFR 255.0(b), (d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)
- The Guides treat endorsements and testimonials the same way. Source: [16 CFR 255.0(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)
- Fictional characters. Example 2 is a TV ad in which two "unidentified shoppers" discuss a detergent: "This obviously fictional dramatization would not be an endorsement." Source: [16 CFR 255.0(g)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)
- An actor reading a script about personal experience ("the most effective ... I have ever tried") is still an endorsement, because consumers are likely to believe it reflects the actor's views. Source: [16 CFR 255.0(g)(6)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)
- Older audiences. Example 10 is an ad aimed at older consumers that uses actors presented as real users. Whether the "actors" disclosure is clear and conspicuous "will be evaluated from the perspective of older consumers, including those with diminished auditory, visual, or cognitive processing abilities." Source: [16 CFR 255.0(g)(10)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)
- Clear and conspicuous means "difficult to miss (i.e., easily noticeable) and easily understandable by ordinary consumers." On the internet, "the disclosure should be unavoidable." It "should not be contradicted or mitigated by, or inconsistent with, anything else in the communication." When an ad targets older adults, "ordinary consumers" includes them. Source: [16 CFR 255.0(f)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)
- Endorsements "must reflect the honest opinions, findings, beliefs, or experience of the endorser." An endorsement "may not convey any express or implied representation that would be deceptive if made directly by the advertiser." Source: [16 CFR 255.1(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.1)
- Exact words. The ad need not use the endorser's exact words "unless the advertisement represents that it is presenting the endorser's exact words, such as through the use of quotation marks." It "may not be presented out of context or reworded so as to distort in any way the endorser's opinion or experience." Source: [16 CFR 255.1(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.1)
- An old post may stay up if the date of the post "is clear and conspicuous to viewers." Source: [16 CFR 255.1(h), Example 1(ii)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.1)
- Using an image or likeness of someone other than the actual endorser "is deceptive if it misrepresents a material attribute of the endorser." Source: [16 CFR 255.1(g)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.1)
- The advertiser is liable for misleading statements made through endorsements, and "may be liable for a deceptive endorsement even when the endorser is not liable." Source: [16 CFR 255.1(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.1)
- Typicality. An endorsement "relating the experience of one or more consumers on a central or key attribute of the product will likely be interpreted as representing that the endorser's experience is representative of what consumers will generally achieve." Without substantiation of that, the ad "should clearly and conspicuously disclose the generally expected performance in the depicted circumstances," and the disclosure "must alter the net impression." Source: [16 CFR 255.2(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.2)
- "Results not typical" fails. In the heat pump example, three customers report savings of $100, $125 and $150 a month, but fewer than 20% of buyers save $100 or more. A disclaimer such as "Results not typical" or "These testimonials are based on the experiences of a few people and you are not likely to have similar results" "is insufficient." The FTC tested such disclaimers, and "neither disclosure adequately reduced the communication that the experiences depicted are generally representative." Acceptable forms: "the average homeowner saves $35 per month" or "most families save 10% on their utility bills." Source: [16 CFR 255.2(e), Example 2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.2)
- A mean that outliers inflate is misleading (mean 15 lb, median 8 lb). Source: [16 CFR 255.2(e), Example 4(iv)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.2)
- Ads that present people as "actual consumers" "should utilize actual consumers ... or clearly and conspicuously disclose that the persons in such advertisements are not actual consumers." Source: [16 CFR 255.2(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.2)
- "Hidden camera" example: even if no patron is named, "the net impression conveyed to consumers may well be that these are actual customers. If actors have been employed, this fact should be clearly and conspicuously disclosed." Source: [16 CFR 255.2(e), Example 6](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.2)
- Material connections. A connection that "might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement" and that the audience does not expect "must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously." These "can include a business, family, or personal relationship," payments, free or discounted products, and "the possibility of being paid." Source: [16 CFR 255.5(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.5)

### Inferences
- A real client story told by the brokerage ("A couple from Ohio bought in ... and ...") is an endorsement if readers take it as the client's experience. It must be true, not distort what happened, and use exact words wherever quotation marks appear.
- A story about a result on a central attribute (sold above asking, closed in 12 days, saved a stated amount) will be read as typical. Either show it is typical, or state the generally expected result next to it in plain words. A "results vary" line will not cure it.
- A clearly labelled hypothetical ("Example:") fits the "obviously fictional dramatization" idea, provided nothing on the page contradicts the label. Things that would contradict it: "our client", first-person quotes from named people, a real-looking photo, or "Chapter3 found them a home."
- Because the readers are retirees, the label must be clear to older readers. The FTC says it judges this from their perspective. A small grey "illustrative" footnote at the bottom is weak. A visible "Example:" at the start of the passage, in body-size text, is stronger.
- Every number inside an example is the brokerage's own claim (255.1(a) applies "as if made directly by the advertiser"). The numbers need the same support as any other claim. The repo's fact-ledger rule already does this.
- A story from a relative, employee or agent of the firm needs the relationship disclosed (255.5).

### Gaps
- The Guides do not mention "composite" characters. No primary source was found on a story that blends several real clients. Such a story is not one real person's experience, so it would need a clear label as an example. That is an inference, not a stated rule.
- The FTC staff FAQ ("FTC's Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking") returned 404 at its old URL on 2026-10-10 and could not be checked for staff views on composites or hypotheticals.

## 2. FTC Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465 (effective October 21, 2024)

### Takeaway
The rule bans a business from writing, creating or spreading testimonials that materially misrepresent that the person exists, that they used the service, or what their experience was. The FTC can seek civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation against knowing violators (2026 figure). The FTC said it is not reasonable to treat "an obviously fictional dramatization" as a testimonial, and it narrowed the rule to "material misrepresentations." A clearly labelled hypothetical is therefore very likely outside the rule. No rule text exempts it by name.

### Cited Findings
- The final rule was published at 89 FR 68034 ff. on August 22, 2024 (rule text at 89 FR 68077) and took effect October 21, 2024. Sources: [Federal Register 2024-18519](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/08/22/2024-18519/trade-regulation-rule-on-the-use-of-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials); [eCFR 16 CFR Part 465](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465); effective date per [Morgan Lewis summary, secondary](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/08/ftc-issues-final-rule-on-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials)
- Definition: a "consumer testimonial" is "an advertising or promotional message ... that consumers are likely to believe reflects the opinions, beliefs, or experiences of a consumer who has purchased, used, or otherwise had experience with a product, service, or business." Source: [16 CFR 465.1(f)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-465.1)
- Core ban, 465.2(a): a business may not "write, create, or sell a consumer review, consumer testimonial, or celebrity testimonial that materially misrepresents, expressly or by implication: (1) That the reviewer or testimonialist exists; (2) That the reviewer or testimonialist used or otherwise had experience with the product, service, or business ...; or (3) The reviewer's or testimonialist's experience." Source: [16 CFR 465.2(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-465.2)
- 465.2(b) also bans disseminating a testimonial the business "knew or should have known" materially misrepresented any of those three things. Source: [16 CFR 465.2(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-465.2)
- Other bans: buying reviews of a set sentiment (465.4); undisclosed insider testimonials by officers, managers, employees or agents (465.5); company-controlled "independent" review sites (465.6); review suppression by threats, or hiding negative reviews while implying all are shown (465.7); fake social media indicators (465.8). Source: [eCFR 16 CFR Part 465](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465)
- Clear and conspicuous under this rule: online, the disclosure "must be unavoidable," and it is not clear "if a consumer must take any action, such as clicking on a hyperlink or hovering over an icon, to see it." When a practice targets "the elderly," "ordinary consumers" includes that group. Source: [16 CFR 465.1(c)(4), (8)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-465.1)
- Dramatizations. A trade group worried that "a clearly dramatized television commercial" could count as a testimonial. The FTC answered: "The Commission agrees that it would not be reasonable for viewers to consider 'an obviously fictional dramatization' to be an endorsement," citing 255.0(g)(2). It kept the definition unchanged because "the concept of 'reasonable consumers' ... is incorporated into the concept of consumers being likely to believe something." Source: [89 FR preamble, Consumer Testimonial definition (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm)
- Material misrepresentation only. Commenters said the proposed ban could reach satire and "virtual influencers" (computer-generated fictional characters). The FTC responded that "Sec. 465.2 is limited to prohibiting material misrepresentations" to "avoid this unintended consequence." Source: [89 FR preamble, Sec. 465.2 discussion (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm)
- Actors. "The provision does not prohibit using an actor to portray a real testimonialist." "Experience" means "what actually happened when they used or otherwise experienced it and not simply their 'opinion' of it." Source: [89 FR preamble (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm)
- AI. The FTC's record cites "reporting regarding the use of generative artificial intelligence ('AI') tools that make it easier for bad actors to write fake reviews." Source: [89 FR preamble (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm). Law firm summaries say the rule expressly covers fake reviews and testimonials made with generative AI. Source: [Hunton, secondary](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-final-rule-targeting-fake-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials-including-those-generated-by-artificial-intelligence-ai-and-online-bots)
- Penalties. The FTC can seek civil penalties for a rule violation "only by showing that a defendant had 'actual knowledge or knowledge fairly implied on the basis of objective circumstances that such act is unfair or deceptive and is prohibited by such rule.'" Courts decide how violations are counted and "may impose much lower per-violation penalties." The rule also gives a faster path to consumer redress under Section 19. Source: [89 FR preamble (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm)
- The maximum penalty under 15 U.S.C. 45(m)(1)(A) is $53,088 per violation. Source: [16 CFR 1.98(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-1.98). The FTC said the amounts "will remain unchanged during 2026," so the 2025 figure still applies. Source: [FTC notice, FR Doc. 2026-18853, published 2026-09-15](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-18853.pdf)
- When the rule was adopted, the figure was $51,744. Source: [89 FR preamble (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm)

### Inferences
- A story presented as a real client's experience that did not happen falls inside 465.2(a). The firm "wrote or created" a testimonial that misrepresents that the person exists or what they experienced. The firm knows it is invented, so the knowledge test for penalties is met. This is the highest-risk mistake in this whole area.
- A story that is real but embellished (a detail added, a timeline shortened, a number rounded up) can misrepresent "the testimonialist's experience" under 465.2(a)(3). The repo rule "never add a detail" to a story-bank story matches this.
- A labelled hypothetical is very likely outside the rule. The FTC's own words say an obviously fictional dramatization is not reasonably taken as a testimonial, and the ban reaches only material misrepresentation. This depends on the label being clear to the target audience (older readers) and on nothing that contradicts it. The rule has no named safe harbor, so this is a reading of the preamble, not an exemption in the text.
- A request to write examples "as if it was a real thing that Chapter3 did," with the label removed, would turn an example into a fake testimonial. Keeping the label is the line between legal and illegal.

### Gaps
- No FTC enforcement action under Part 465 against a real estate brokerage was found. Enforcement practice on labelled hypotheticals is untested.
- The rule does not define how prominent a label on a written hypothetical must be. That comes only from the general "clear and conspicuous" definition.

## 3. FTC Act Section 5: deception standard, dramatizations and illustrative examples

### Takeaway
Section 5 asks whether the net impression on a reasonable member of the target audience is misleading in a material way. A dramatization or example is lawful if the audience understands it is not a real event, and if the claims inside it (prices, savings, timelines) are true and supported.

### Cited Findings
- The Endorsement Guides apply Section 5 of the FTC Act (15 U.S.C. 45) to endorsements. "Whether a particular endorsement or testimonial is deceptive will depend on the specific factual circumstances of the advertisement at issue." Source: [16 CFR 255.0(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)
- The FTC Policy Statement on Deception is dated October 14, 1983. Source: [FTC legal library page](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-deception). The FTC cites it for the "reasonable consumers" concept that sits inside "likely to believe." Source: [89 FR preamble, footnote 155 (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm)
- The Guides use "net impression" as the test: a disclosure "must alter the net impression of the advertisement so that it is not misleading," and the "hidden camera" example turns on "the net impression conveyed to consumers." Source: [16 CFR 255.2(b), Example 6](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.2)
- A disclosure must not be "contradicted or mitigated by, or inconsistent with, anything else in the communication." Source: [16 CFR 255.0(f)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)
- The guidance names "obviously fictional dramatization" as the case that is not an endorsement, and says actors presented as real customers need a clear disclosure. Sources: [16 CFR 255.0(g)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0); [16 CFR 255.2(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.2)

### Inferences
- An example labelled "Example" and written in the company voice is low risk under Section 5 if three things hold. (1) The label is visible where the example starts. (2) No line says or implies the people are clients. (3) Every fact and number in it is true for the market described.
- An example can still deceive through its numbers. A hypothetical buyer who "pays $2,100 a year in taxes" makes a real claim about real taxes. It needs a source, like any other claim.
- If the example shows what the firm does for buyers ("a Chapter3 agent reads the HOA rules with them"), that is a service claim. It must be something the firm actually does.

### Gaps
- The full PDF of the 1983 Deception Policy Statement returned 404 at its old FTC URL on 2026-10-10, so its text was not quoted here.
- No FTC guidance was found that addresses written hypotheticals or "worked examples" in blogs. The analysis rests on the dramatization language above.

## 4. South Carolina Real Estate Commission rules and NAR Code of Ethics Article 12

### Takeaway
South Carolina has no statute or regulation that names testimonials. The license law punishes false promises, misrepresentation, and a "continued and flagrant course of misrepresentation" in advertising, and it requires the brokerage name in every ad. New SC advertising regulations (Regulation Document 5437) were submitted in March 2026, and their status is unclear. The NAR Code (Article 12) requires a "true picture" in advertising, but it binds only REALTOR members.

### Cited Findings
- Disciplinary grounds: a licensee may be disciplined who "(2) makes a substantial misrepresentation involving a real estate transaction; (3) makes false promises likely to influence, persuade, or induce; (4) pursues a continued and flagrant course of misrepresentation or makes false and misleading promises through any medium of advertising or otherwise; (5) in the practice of real estate, demonstrates bad faith, dishonesty, untrustworthiness, or incompetency in a manner as to endanger the interest of the public." Source: [S.C. Code 40-57-710(A)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c057.php)
- Another ground is a licensee who "guarantees or authorizes and permits a supervised licensee to guarantee future profits from the resale of real estate." Source: [S.C. Code 40-57-710(A)(7)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c057.php)
- A conviction for violating federal or state fair housing laws is also a ground. Source: [S.C. Code 40-57-710(A)(9)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c057.php)
- Advertising identity: "When advertising his real estate services ... in any medium, a licensee clearly must: (a) Identify the full name of the real estate brokerage firm." Online, a link to the firm's homepage can meet this. A trade or franchise name must reveal the holder. Source: [S.C. Code 40-57-135(E)(2), (3)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c057.php)
- A licensee may not advertise property owned by someone else without a written listing agreement, or written authorization from the listing firm with the firm acknowledged in the ad. Source: [S.C. Code 40-57-135(E)(1)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c057.php). The Commission issued a reminder in June 2025 after "an increasing number of complaints" about unauthorized use of listings and property images. Source: [SC LLR, Reminder of Advertising Restrictions (PDF)](https://llr.sc.gov/re/News/Reminder%20of%20Advertising%20Restrictions.pdf)
- Team ads must show the full brokerage name, and the team name may not be more than twice the size, prominence or frequency of the firm name. Source: [S.C. Code 40-57 (team provisions)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c057.php)
- Pending regulations: S. 1073 would approve Regulation Document 5437, which covers "licensee supervision, advertising, and teams." It was introduced March 31, 2026 and is "Currently residing in the Senate Committee on Labor, Commerce and Industry." Source: [SC Legislature, Bill 1073](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/sess126_2025-2026/bills/1073.htm)
- NAR Code, 2026 edition, effective January 1, 2026. Article 12: "REALTORS® shall be honest and truthful in their real estate communications and shall present a true picture in their advertising, marketing, and other representations." Source: [NAR 2026 Code of Ethics](https://www.nar.realtor/about-nar/governing-documents/code-of-ethics/2026-code-of-ethics-standards-of-practice)
- NAR Standards of Practice relevant to stories: 12-7 lets only the listing or cooperating broker in a deal claim to have "sold" a property. 12-8 says to "use reasonable efforts to ensure that information on their websites is current." 12-10 bans "misleading consumers, including use of misleading images." 12-13 allows only credentials actually held. Article 12 and its Standards contain no standard on testimonials. Source: [NAR 2026 Code of Ethics](https://www.nar.realtor/about-nar/governing-documents/code-of-ethics/2026-code-of-ethics-standards-of-practice)
- NAR Standard of Practice 10-3 bars ads that indicate a preference based on the Article 10 classes. Those classes include "sexual orientation, or gender identity," which is broader than the federal Fair Housing Act. Source: [NAR 2026 Code of Ethics](https://www.nar.realtor/about-nar/governing-documents/code-of-ethics/2026-code-of-ethics-standards-of-practice)

### Inferences
- In South Carolina, the FTC rules are the specific limit on testimonials. The state's license law adds discipline for misrepresentation and false promises. An invented client story is a misrepresentation in advertising. A story that implies guaranteed appreciation ("they'll make 30% when they sell") could fall under 40-57-710(A)(3) or (A)(7).
- Every story page must carry the full brokerage name (40-57-135(E)(2)). Site-wide headers or footers with the firm name likely meet this. Under the DBA, the holder of the trade name must be revealed (E)(3).
- A story must not say "we sold" a home unless the firm was the listing or cooperating broker (NAR 12-7, if a REALTOR member). It must not use a photo of a property the firm has no authority to show (40-57-135(E)(1)).
- Old stories need review. NAR 12-8 and FTC 255.1(h) both point to keeping dated, current content.

### Gaps
- No current SC Real Estate Commission regulation text on advertising was found. Justia returned 403 and the scstatehouse.gov Chapter 105 URL returned 404. The content of Regulation Document 5437 was not found. Under the SC Administrative Procedures Act, regulations can take effect without an approving resolution after a set period. Whether Document 5437 has done so was not checked. Ask SC LLR or check the State Register.
- Whether Chapter3 or its agents are NAR members was not checked. Article 12 binds only members.
- SC fine amounts for licensee discipline (S.C. Code 40-1-120) were not fetched.

## 5. Fair Housing Act advertising and stories on a 55+ community page

### Takeaway
Any ad or statement about selling a dwelling may not indicate a preference or limitation based on race, color, religion, sex, handicap (disability), familial status or national origin. Age as such is not a federal class. A HOPA-qualified 55+ community is exempt from the familial-status rules and may advertise its age limit. A story may describe the example people's ages and the community's real age rule. It must not express a preference for kinds of people ("perfect for empty nesters", "no kids", "healthy, active adults", "Christian neighbors", ethnic labels). HUD has withdrawn some advertising guidance since 2025, but the statute and 24 CFR 100.75 are unchanged.

### Cited Findings
- "It shall be unlawful to make, print or publish ... any notice, statement or advertisement with respect to the sale or rental of a dwelling which indicates any preference, limitation or discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin." This mirrors 42 U.S.C. 3604(c). Source: [24 CFR 100.75(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.75)
- The rule applies to "all written or oral notices or statements by a person engaged in the sale or rental of a dwelling." Banned content includes "words, phrases, photographs, illustrations, symbols or forms which convey that dwellings are available or not available to a particular group of persons." Source: [24 CFR 100.75(b), (c)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.75)
- It is also discriminatory to select "media or locations for advertising ... which deny particular segments of the housing market information about housing opportunities" on a protected basis. Source: [24 CFR 100.75(c)(3)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.75)
- 100.75(d) still refers to "24 CFR part 109" advertising guidelines, but Part 109 does not appear in the current Title 24 structure (eCFR structure checked 2026-10-10). Only Part 100 was found. Source: [eCFR Title 24 structure](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24)
- 55+ housing (HOPA): at least 80 percent of occupied units must have at least one occupant 55 or older. Sources: [24 CFR 100.304](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.304); [24 CFR 100.305(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.305)
- The community "must publish and adhere to policies and procedures that demonstrate its intent to operate as housing for persons 55 years of age or older." Relevant factors include "The manner in which the housing facility or community is described to prospective residents" and "Any advertising designed to attract prospective residents." Source: [24 CFR 100.306(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.306)
- "Phrases such as 'adult living', 'adult community', or similar statements in any written advertisement or prospectus are not consistent with the intent that the housing facility or community intends to operate as housing for persons 55 years of age or older." Source: [24 CFR 100.306(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.306)
- South Carolina has its own ad ban with the same classes. It is unlawful "to make, print, or publish ... any notice, statement, or advertisement with respect to the sale or rental of a dwelling that indicates any preference, limitation, or discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin." Source: [S.C. Code 31-21-40(3)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t31c021.php)
- SC's definition of 55+ "housing for older persons" still lists "the existence of significant facilities and services specifically designed to meet the physical or social needs of older persons" as a factor. Federal rules do not list that factor (see 24 CFR 100.304 to 100.306). Source: [S.C. Code 31-21-30(8)(c)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t31c021.php)
- The 1995 Achtenberg memo is HUD's January 9, 1995 memorandum "Guidance Regarding Advertisements Under Section 804(c)." On religion it says ads "should not contain an explicit preference ... on account of religion (i.e., no Jews, Christian home)." A religious name or symbol "standing alone, may indicate a religious preference." "Advertisements containing descriptions of properties (apartment complex with chapel), or services (kosher meals available) do not on their face state a preference." Source: [City of Spokane fair housing handout quoting the memo, secondary](https://static.spokanecity.org/documents/blog/2015/04/06/register-for-the-2015-inland-northwest-fair-housing-conference/advertising-and-the-internet.pdf)
- The same handout lists words that "could be discriminatory." The list includes "adult community", "adult living", "retirees", "senior citizen(s)", "empty nesters", "adults only", "mature couple", "healthy only" and "able-bodied." It marks "senior adult community" and "senior citizen(s)" as allowed "only when complex or development qualifies as housing for older persons." Listed as not discriminatory: "Families welcome", "Gated community", "Golf course, nearby", "Quiet neighborhood", "Swimming pool". Source: [City of Spokane handout, secondary](https://static.spokanecity.org/documents/blog/2015/04/06/register-for-the-2015-inland-northwest-fair-housing-conference/advertising-and-the-internet.pdf)
- A HUD legal opinion (GME-0008) says words in each ad are examined in context. Source: [HUD Legal Opinion GME-0008, per search summary](https://www.hud.gov/sites/documents/gme-0008lops.pdf)
- HUD withdrawal notices. HUD withdrew its April 29, 2024 "Guidance on Application of the Fair Housing Act to the Advertising of Housing ... through Digital Platforms," effective September 17, 2025, with seven other FHEO documents. The notice says they "have been removed from the HUD.gov website and should not be relied upon." It also states: "Any actions that do not comply with the text of the Fair Housing Act continue to be subject to enforcement." Source: [FR Doc. 2026-06624, April 6, 2026](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-06624.pdf)
- HUD's Office of General Counsel withdrew 13 more documents, effective September 25, 2025, in a notice of July 17, 2026. Source: [FR Doc. 2026-14432](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-17/pdf/2026-14432.pdf)
- The 1995 Achtenberg memo is not named in either withdrawal table. Its hud.gov URL (DOC_7784.PDF) returned 404 on 2026-10-10. Sources: [FR Doc. 2026-06624](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-06624.pdf); [FR Doc. 2026-14432](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-17/pdf/2026-14432.pdf)
- Commentators describe the memo as internal guidance to HUD staff, not guidance to brokers. Source: [Oregon REALTORS, secondary, via search summary](https://oregonrealtors.org/news/tip-of-the-week/permissible-advertisements-under-the-fair-housing-act/)

### Inferences
- What a story on a 55+ community page may say:
  - The people's ages and life stage as facts about them ("Example: Ruth, 67, and Don, 70, are selling their house in Pennsylvania").
  - The community's real age rule, if the community qualifies under HOPA ("at least one buyer must be 55 or older").
  - Amenities and places ("clubhouse", "pickleball courts", "chapel on site", "near the hospital", "golf nearby").
- What it may not say or imply:
  - A preference for or against families with children outside a qualified 55+ community ("no kids around", "quiet adults").
  - Religion as a preference ("Christian neighbors", "near our church" as a selling point).
  - Race, ethnicity or national origin of residents or neighbors.
  - Disability ("perfect for healthy, active adults", "not for wheelchair users").
  - "Adult community" or "adult living" as the label for a 55+ community, because 100.306(b) treats those phrases as inconsistent with 55+ status.
- Describing where buyers move from by state ("from New Jersey") is not national origin. Ethnic or foreign-origin labels would be.
- The repo already labels 55+ communities by their legal status. Before a story calls a place "55+", the HOPA status should be a verified fact row. Describing a non-qualified neighborhood as "for retirees" is the risk case under familial status.
- Stock photos in stories should not show only one race or only one kind of household across all pages. HUD's older human-model guidance was in Part 109, which is no longer in eCFR. This rests on the general "photographs, illustrations" language in 100.75(c)(1).
- NAR members also must not show a preference by sexual orientation or gender identity (SOP 10-3).

### Gaps
- The full text of the 1995 Achtenberg memo could not be retrieved from a primary source. hud.gov returns 404 and the archive was unreachable. Its familial-status and disability passages are not quoted here. Whether HUD still treats it as guidance is unclear.
- How SC's Human Affairs Commission applies the "significant facilities and services" factor in 31-21-30(8)(c) today was not checked. A community could qualify federally but face questions under SC's definition.
- No recent HUD or DOJ enforcement action about blog stories or "buyer persona" examples was found.

## 6. Google: E-E-A-T "Experience", helpful content, scaled content abuse, AI-generated content

### Takeaway
Google asks for first-hand experience and original value, and its rater guidelines rate fake testimonials and made-up creator profiles as Lowest. A labelled hypothetical can be helpful, but it does not show first-hand experience. Real stories from the firm's own files do. Mass-produced pages, with or without AI, can trigger the scaled content abuse policy, which Google enforces with some site-wide signals.

### Cited Findings
- Rater Guidelines, September 11, 2025, Section 3.4, "Experience": "Consider the extent to which the content creator has the necessary first-hand or life experience for the topic. Many types of pages are trustworthy and achieve their purpose well when created by people with a wealth of personal experience. For example, which would you trust: a product review from someone who has personally used the product or a 'review' by someone who has not?" Source: [Search Quality Evaluator Guidelines PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/guidelines.raterhub.com/en//searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf)
- Section 3.4.1, YMYL Topics, Experience or Expertise: "Pages that share first-hand life experience on clear YMYL topics may be considered to have high E-E-A-T as long as the content is trustworthy, safe, and consistent with well-established expert consensus. In contrast, some types of YMYL information and advice must come from experts." Its table says "Reviews of retirement saving services by people with first-hand experience" are valuable, while "Advice on how to invest for retirement" is "best left to Experts." Source: [Rater Guidelines PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/guidelines.raterhub.com/en//searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf)
- Section 4.5.3, Deceptive Information: examples rated Lowest include a page that "may have false testimonials for a product," a site with "'fake' owner or content creator profiles. For example, AI generated content with made up 'author' profiles," and "Factually inaccurate and deceptive information about creator expertise." Source: [Rater Guidelines PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/guidelines.raterhub.com/en//searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf)
- Section 4.6.5, Scaled Content Abuse: pages "created at scale with no original content or added value for users, should be rated Lowest, no matter how they are created." Source: [Rater Guidelines PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/guidelines.raterhub.com/en//searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf)
- The Rater Guidelines also say: "the use of Generative AI tools alone does not determine the level of effort or Page Quality rating." Source: [Rater Guidelines PDF, section 4.6.6](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/guidelines.raterhub.com/en//searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf)
- Helpful content guidance, last updated 2026-10-05. It asks: "Does your content clearly demonstrate first-hand expertise and a depth of knowledge ...?" It says of E-E-A-T, "Of these aspects, trust is most important." It gives "a person sharing their experience about finding a new way to clear their driveway of snow" as experience that "doesn't necessarily require specialized expertise." Source: [Google, Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)
- The same page, on "How": "That can include automated, AI-generated, and AI-assisted content." "Is the use of automation, including AI-generation, self-evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways?" Source: [Google, Creating helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)
- Google's AI optimization guide, last updated 2026-07-10: "a first-hand review provides a unique perspective based on personal experience." It asks for content that "provides unique expert or experienced takes that go beyond common knowledge and the ordinary." Source: [Google AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)
- "Hidden gems": in November 2023, Google said it surfaces content in which people share "first-hand knowledge and their own personal insights and experiences," and that this is part of core ranking. Sources: [Search Engine Land, secondary](https://searchengineland.com/google-search-ranking-improvement-aims-to-surface-hidden-gems-434623); [Search Engine Roundtable quoting Google SearchLiaison, secondary](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-search-hidden-gems-ranking-algorithm-36388.html)
- Spam policies, last updated 2026-08-28. "Scaled content abuse is when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users ... no matter how it's created." Examples include "Using generative AI tools or other similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users" and "Stitching or combining content from different web pages without adding value." Source: [Google spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)
- March 5, 2024: Google introduced the scaled content abuse policy so it can act "no matter whether content is produced through automation, human efforts, or some combination." The same post says: "Our core ranking systems are primarily designed to work on the page level ... We do have some site-wide signals that are also considered." Source: [Google Search Central Blog, March 2024 core update and new spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2024/03/core-update-spam-policies)
- Generative AI guidance, last updated 2026-10-01: "It is critical to manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content for accuracy and trustworthiness before publishing." If content is generated automatically, "consider adding information on how your content was created." Source: [Google, Using generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content)

### Inferences
- For search, the strongest story is a real one from the firm's own files, told with specific, checkable details. That is "first-hand experience" in Google's sense.
- A labelled hypothetical is a teaching tool. It shows the arithmetic and the decision. It can make a page more helpful, but it is not "experience." Google does not say a hypothetical hurts. It does not say one counts as experience either.
- An unlabelled invented story is worse than none on both fronts. It is a fake testimonial under FTC 465 and a "false testimonials" Lowest signal under Rater Guidelines 4.5.3.
- Home buying and retirement money are YMYL topics. A story can carry experience ("what the first summer in the condo was like"). Advice on loans, taxes or investments inside a story needs expert backing or a source.
- If AI drafts the stories, Google asks for human fact-checking. It also asks site owners to consider saying how content was made.

### Gaps
- Google has not published how much a single page's "experience" content affects ranking. Rater Guidelines guide human raters and are not ranking signals themselves.
- No Google statement addresses labelled hypothetical examples specifically.

## 7. Answer engines: how passages get selected and quoted

### Takeaway
Google AI Overviews and AI Mode use the normal index and snippet rules, with "query fan-out" over subtopics. Google says its systems can show "the relevant piece" of a page. Microsoft says Copilot and Bing split pages into pieces and lift "self-contained" sentences that "make sense even when pulled out of context." OpenAI documents only crawler access. Perplexity's help page could not be read. No official source or credible study was found on whether stories or examples get cited more or less than other text.

### Cited Findings
- Google AI features page, last updated 2025-12-10. "There are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary." A page "must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet." Both features may use "query fan-out," meaning "issuing multiple related searches across subtopics and data sources." Owners can limit display with "nosnippet, data-nosnippet, max-snippet, or noindex." Source: [Google, AI features and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)
- Google AI optimization guide: "Google systems are able to understand the nuance of multiple topics on a page and show the relevant piece to users." "There's no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces for AI to better understand it." "You don't need to write in a specific way just for generative AI search." "Our AI systems take a look at a variety of sources, so it can be helpful to have a unique viewpoint that stands out." Source: [Google AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)
- Microsoft (Krishna Madhavan, October 8, 2025): "AI assistants don't read a page top to bottom like a person would. They break content into smaller, usable pieces." "AI can lift a single line or combine multiple snippets from across sites into one answer." It recommends "Self-contained phrasing: Sentences that make sense even when pulled out of context" and "Concise answers: One- to two-sentence responses that directly address a question." It says to "anchor claims in measurable facts" and gives "42 dB dishwasher designed for open-concept kitchens" instead of "quiet dishwasher." The post does not mention stories, anecdotes or first-hand experience. Source: [Microsoft Advertising blog](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/october-2025/optimizing-your-content-for-inclusion-in-ai-search-answers)
- OpenAI: "OAI-SearchBot is used to surface websites in search results in ChatGPT's search features." "Sites that are opted out of OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers." The bots page says nothing on how sources are chosen. Source: [OpenAI crawlers](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots)
- GEO experiment (Aggarwal et al., KDD 2024): adding quotations, statistics and source citations raised visibility in generative engine answers. Storytelling was not one of the tested methods. Source: [arXiv 2311.09735](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735); figures as recorded in this repo's evidence file (`research/seo-aeo-evidence-2026-10.md`), not re-read for this note.
- Kevin Indig's study of 1.2M ChatGPT answers found 44.2% of citations come from the first 30% of the page. Cited passages use definitive language and many named entities. Source: [Search Engine Land, secondary](https://searchengineland.com/chatgpt-citations-content-study-469483), as recorded in `research/seo-aeo-evidence-2026-10.md`
- On this site, a search engine's AI summary lifted the first sentence of /invest/str-vs-ltr/ verbatim (SERP check, 2026-10-01). Source: `research/seo-aeo-evidence-2026-10.md` (internal repo record)

### Inferences
- Answer engines lift single sentences. A label that appears only once ("Example:") at the start of a story can be lost when a later sentence is quoted alone. A quoted line like "Ruth and Don paid $1,900 in property tax on their Myrtle Beach condo" could then read as a real case.
- Safer pattern: keep the example's teaching point in self-contained sentences that carry their own frame ("In this example, a $300,000 condo at the 4% rate pays about ..."). Put the real, sourced fact in its own plain sentence outside the narrative, so engines quote the fact, not the character.
- Real stories from the firm's files give the "unique viewpoint" Google asks for. Quote-ready details include a named place, a dated rule, and a specific step the firm takes. These are the parts most likely to be lifted, so each must be exactly true.
- If a story must never be quoted alone, `data-nosnippet` on that block stops Google from showing it in snippets and AI features. The cost is that the block cannot be cited. Use it only for passages where the out-of-context risk is high.
- Stories should sit below the answer-first opening. The evidence on citations points to the top of the page and to direct, definitive sentences.

### Gaps
- No official documentation or credible study was found on whether answer engines cite narrative examples or anecdotes, or how they treat a labelled hypothetical. This is the main open question for AEO value.
- Perplexity's help-center article returned 403. Only third-party descriptions of Perplexity's ranking were found, and they are not used here.
- OpenAI's ChatGPT search help page returned 403. No OpenAI statement on source selection was read.

## 8. Cadence: publishing frequency and volume

### Takeaway
Google says publishing frequency is not a ranking factor. It warns against producing lots of content on many topics, heavy automation, writing to a word count, and adding or removing many pages to look fresh. No credible source gives a safe number of pages per month for a small site.

### Cited Findings
- Google's helpful content self-check asks: "Are you producing lots of content on many different topics in hopes that some of it might perform well in search results?" "Are you using extensive automation to produce content on many topics?" "Are you writing to a particular word count?" "Is the content mass-produced by or outsourced to a large number of creators?" "Are you adding a lot of new content or removing a lot of older content" (to seem fresh). Source: [Google, Creating helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)
- Google AI optimization guide: creating separate content for every search variation is discouraged, "as a high quantity of pages doesn't make a website higher quality or more relevant to users," and doing so at scale "violates Google's scaled content abuse spam policy." The guide gives no publishing frequency. Source: [Google AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)
- Google office-hours, September 2022, speaker unnamed: "Posting daily or at any specific frequency for that matter doesn't help with ranking better in Google search results." Also: "the more pages you have in the Google index, the more your content may show up in search results." Source: [Search Engine Journal, secondary](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/does-adding-content-daily-increase-rankings/)
- John Mueller (via SEJ): "The other big reason why we don't crawl a lot from websites is because we're not convinced about the quality overall." Source: [Search Engine Journal, secondary](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/does-adding-content-daily-increase-rankings/)
- Google uses "some site-wide signals" alongside page-level ranking. Source: [Google Search Central Blog, March 2024](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2024/03/core-update-spam-policies)
- The spam policy applies "no matter how it's created." Source: [Google spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)

### Inferences
- Cadence should follow how fast the line can produce pages that pass the standard, with verified facts, a real or labelled story, and owner review. A calendar target should not set it.
- Many near-identical story pages ("Retiring to [town] from [state]") with swapped names and numbers would look like the scaled pattern Google describes. Each story should carry facts specific to that page's topic.
- Because some signals are site-wide, a batch of weak pages can affect the whole site. A small site gains more from fewer, stronger pages and from upgrading old ones (Lane U) than from volume.

### Gaps
- No credible study was found that sets a safe page count or publishing rate for a small local site. Vendor claims on this were not used.
- Google's own wording on frequency is secondhand (SEJ reporting of an office-hours video). No Search Central documentation page on posting frequency was found.
