# Simple stories: what makes a 5 to 8 sentence story easy to follow in one read

Scope: why the owner called the Del Webb at Grande Dunes example "impossible to understand", and what makes a very short story easy to follow for readers 55 to 75 on a phone. Gathered 2026-10-11. It adds to `story_craft.md` (structure, Pixar, Heath, Dicks) and does not repeat it.

Grades: **[T]** tested in a study. **[E]** expert or practitioner opinion. **[I]** my inference for this site.

House note: no em dashes; "Source:" introduces each source.

## 1. What went wrong in the Grande Dunes example

The example asked the reader to do five things at once:

1. Hold a rule with three parts: a guest, under 19, 90 nights in any 12 months in a row.
2. Hold two future visits (Christmas, then the next summer) and see that they fall in one 12-month window.
3. Count nights and compare the total with 90.
4. Follow a change of date ("moves the boys' summer arrival to the first week of July") and a second count.
5. Follow a sentence about what did not happen ("If the visits had not fit, they could have...").

Each of these is a separate item to keep in mind. Sections 2 to 5 show why that is too many. **[I]**

## 2. A reader can hold about four things at once

- **[T]** Nelson Cowan reviewed the evidence on short-term memory and concluded that "a single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks is implicated." Miller's "seven" was "more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit." Source: [Cowan, "The magical number 4 in short-term memory", Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24 (2001), author's copy](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf)
- **[T]** Reasoning with "if" gets harder as the number of possibilities a reader must picture goes up. Barrouillet and Lecas report that a deduction "would be all the harder the higher the number of models they require", and that people with a larger working memory span build more of them. Source: [Barrouillet and Lecas, "Mental models in conditional reasoning and working memory", Thinking and Reasoning (1999), PhilPapers record; quote taken from the abstract as shown in search, the page itself refused the fetch](https://philpapers.org/rec/BARMMI)

**[I]** The people, their want, the problem and the fix already fill four slots. A rule with three parts, a second date, a count or an "if" pushes the story past what a reader can hold in one pass.

## 3. Readers remember the chain of causes that starts with a goal

- **[T]** In story recall studies, "statements that are on the causal chain tend to be recalled more frequently", and events with more causal connections are "recalled more often and more quickly". The chain "begins with statements that introduce the protagonists and their goals." Source: [Cevasco and van den Broek, "Contributions of Causality Processing Models...", Psicología Educativa 25(2), 2019](https://journals.copmadrid.org/psed/art/psed2019a8), reviewing Trabasso and van den Broek (1985) and Black and Bower (1980).
- **[T]** The event-indexing model (Zwaan, Langston and Graesser, Psychological Science, 1995) says readers link events on "five dimensions: time, space, entity, causation, and motivation": when, where, who, why it happened, and what the character wants. A change on any of them makes the reader update. Source: [Zwaan, "Situation models, mental simulations, and abstract concepts in discourse comprehension", Psychonomic Bulletin and Review 23, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4974264); I did not read the 1995 paper.
- **[E]** The story grammar of Stein and Glenn (1979) breaks one episode into a setting, a starting event, the character's goal, an attempt, and a result. Source: [University at Buffalo history of communicative disorders note](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/history-of-cds/?p=1395)

**[I]** One goal gives the reader one chain to follow. A second goal (a second visit, a second plan) starts a second chain. Every time jump or new date is an update the reader must make.

## 4. Older readers lean on the gist, and fewer of them like numbers

- **[T]** Sixty highly educated adults read three texts. The 60 to 80 group did worse than the 20 to 40 group on recall of detail, on the main structure of a text, and on working memory tasks. A subgroup of older readers matched the young. Source: [Chesneau, Jbabdi, Champagne-Lavau, Giroux and Ska, "Text comprehension, cognitive resources and aging", 2007, Oxford ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:eeed4ae2-460b-4425-825c-efc5e6f5bf53)
- **[T]** "Older adults prefer gist information and gist-based information processing," while "younger adults favor verbatim information." Source: [Mei, Nolte and Löckenhoff, Innovation in Aging 7 (Suppl 1), 2023, conference abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10738971/). The full study is Nolte, Löckenhoff and Reyna, Psychology and Aging 37(2), 2022, doi 10.1037/pag0000676; I read only summaries of it.
- **[T]** In 306 UK adults, "Older age was related to lower numeracy and lower need for cognition," after controlling for education. Source: [Bruine de Bruin et al., "Thinking about numbers is not my idea of fun", Medical Decision Making, 2014](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/80675/)
- **[T]** US adults aged 55 to 65 scored lower in numeracy in 2023 than in 2017. Source: [AIR, Highlights of the U.S. PIAAC Cycle 2 results](https://air.org/resource/report/highlights-us-piaac-cycle-2-results)

**[I]** The gist of the Grande Dunes problem is "the rules only let grandkids stay part of the year." The story gave the verbatim rule and asked the reader to compute. Older readers keep the gist, so the story should give the gist and leave the exact rule to the FAQ above it.

## 5. Phones make hard text harder

- **[T]** In a cloze test on privacy policies, readers scored 39% on a desktop screen and 19% on an iPhone-sized screen. A text is "easy" at 60% or more. Source: [Nielsen, "Mobile Content Is Twice as Difficult", NN/g, 2011](https://nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content-is-twice-as-difficult-2011)
- **[T]** A larger 2016 study (276 people) found no practical difference on easy or hard articles, but hard passages "took longer on mobile." NN/g still advises "prioritizing brevity and reducing unnecessary content when writing for mobile." Source: [Moran, "Reading Content on Mobile Devices", NN/g, 2016](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/)

**[I]** On a phone the reader sees two or three sentences at a time. A story that needs the reader to scroll back to check a number has failed.

## 6. Fables and picture books: want, problem, fix

- **[E]** Aesop, "The Crow and the Pitcher", in four sentences: a thirsty crow finds a pitcher; the water is too low to reach; he tries everything and fails; he drops in stones until the water rises. One want, one problem, one fix, one picture. "The Fox and the Grapes" is three sentences. Source: [Aesop's Fables, tr. George Fyler Townsend, Project Gutenberg #21](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/21)
- **[E]** Mem Fox, picture-book author: "DO keep the text under 500 words if possible. Minimise description." "DO write with narrative tension: solve a problem." "if there's no trouble, there's no story." "character comes first. Then trouble." "DO constantly re-read drafts aloud." "DO NOT write to teach." Source: [Mem Fox, "Writing Do's and Don'ts"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/twenty-writing-dos-and-donts) and ["Writing Picture Books"](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books)
- **[E]** Kurt Vonnegut, rule 3 of 8 for short stories: "Every character should want something, even if it is only a glass of water." From the preface to *Bagombo Snuff Box* (1999). Source: [reprint, Boing Boing, 2007](https://boingboing.net/2007/04/14/vonneguts-rules-for.html)
- **[E]** Emma Coats's Pixar tips, as reprinted: simplify, merge characters, cut detours (5); find the story's essence and its most economical telling (22). Source: [RedShark News, 2013](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling); more in `story_craft.md` section 1.

**[I]** The crow is the model: the problem is physical and a child sees it at once. "The rules only let grandkids stay part of the year" is the same kind of problem. "90 nights in any 12 months in a row" is not.

## 7. Simple means one core, not fewer words

- **[E]** The Heaths' "Simple" principle is finding the core, which they tie to the Army's "Commander's Intent". A summary quotes them: "If you attempt to say three things, you end up saying nothing." Source: [Actionable Books summary of Made to Stick](https://actionablebooks.com/summaries/made-to-stick); the book text was not available. The curse of knowledge and the tappers study are in `story_craft.md` section 4.
- **[E]** The "explain it to a six-year-old" line is not Einstein's. The closest recorded words are in Ronald Clark's 1972 biography: theories should be simple enough "that even a child could understand them." Source: [wist.info entry](https://wist.info/einstein-albert/15144/); not checked against the book. Do not quote it on the site.
- **[E]** The federal plain language guidelines say to organize so readers do not need cross-references, and that "Examples help your readers understand your points." Source: [PlainLanguage.gov, Minimize cross-references](https://PlainLanguage.gov/guidelines/design/minimize-cross-references/)

**[I]** The curse of knowledge explains the Grande Dunes draft. The writer knew the rule well, so counting nights felt like the interesting part. To the reader it was homework.

## 8. How to test whether a story lands

- **[E]** Paraphrase testing: "Paraphrase testing will tell you what a reader thinks a piece of writing means." Readers read a section and explain it in their own words; "Do not correct the participant"; "wherever participants misunderstood the message, the content has a problem that you should fix." Use 6 to 9 people. Source: [Digital.gov plain language guide, Paraphrase testing](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/test/paraphrase-testing)
- **[E]** Teach-back (AHRQ): ask the person to say in their own words what they need to know or do. It is "not a test of the patient's knowledge. It is a test of how well you explained the concept." Source: [AHRQ Health Literacy Universal Precautions Toolkit, 3rd ed.](https://www.ahrq.gov:443/sites/default/files/publications2/files/health-literacy-universal-precautions-toolkit-3rd-edition.pdf)
- **[E]** The five-second test (show a design briefly, hide it, ask what the person remembers) checks whether the main message comes across. One case study notes the five seconds is a convention, not a tested cutoff. Source: [Smashing Magazine, 2023](https://smashingmagazine.com/2023/12/five-second-testing-case-study)
- **[E]** Read aloud: Mem Fox above. Parker and Stone's "but / therefore" test is in `story_craft.md`.
- **[E]** Journalism: an anecdotal opening must get to the point fast, and critics call long ones a common newsroom problem. Source: [Dick Tofel, "Why starting long news stories with anecdotes may have outlived its usefulness"](https://dicktofel.substack.com/p/why-starting-long-news-stories-with). Scanlan's nut graf rule is in `story_craft.md`.

**[I]** For this site, a full paraphrase test with 6 to 9 people is too slow for every story. A cold retell by one person who has not seen the page, after one read on a phone, catches the worst failures. The writer writes the one-sentence retell first; the checker writes their own without looking; if the two differ on the problem or the fix, the story fails.

## 9. What kills a very short story

Each item is in the Grande Dunes draft or one of the other three drafts.

| Killer | Why | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic or counting | Adds items to memory; numeracy is lower in older adults | [T] Cowan; Bruine de Bruin |
| Rule mechanics (age limits, rolling windows, "on a lot that backs onto X or Y") | The reader must hold the rule's parts and test each case | [T] Barrouillet and Lecas; [I] |
| "If", "unless", "could have" | Each condition is a second picture to hold | [T] Barrouillet and Lecas |
| Two timelines or two visits | Each time change is an update; two goals make two chains | [T] Zwaan et al.; Trabasso |
| Abstract words ("the city's plan", "high-risk zone on the federal map") | Nothing to picture | [E] Heath; repo rule P3 |
| Jargon | The curse of knowledge | [E] Heath; [T] Newton 1990 |
| More than one want | Two causal chains | [E] Vonnegut, Coats; [T] Trabasso |
| A re-count or second check | The story restarts | [I] |
