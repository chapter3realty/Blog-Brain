# Write short stories retiree buyers remember

The best short story for a retiree buyer page follows one buyer or couple through a worry the reader already has. It shows one small moment where they decide and ends on one concrete outcome. It has 5 to 8 plain sentences, and the sentence after it says what it means for the reader. The case for stories is real but modest. Across 33,078 people, readers **remembered stories better than plain explanations by about 0.7 of a standard deviation** ([Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)). Stories changed what people intended to do more than what they believed, and statistics did better at changing beliefs ([Zebregs et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/10410236.2013.842528)). The story should help the reader picture the decision, and the numbers belong in plain sentences outside it. Three popular claims should not be repeated: "22 times more memorable" (no study exists), oxytocin as the reason stories work (the trust finding behind it failed a 677-person registered replication), and the identifiable victim effect (a 2024 replication found no effect). The readers are people 55 to 75 moving mostly from North Carolina, New York, Pennsylvania and Ohio. They worry most about money lasting, being near doctors, leaving family, making friends and sorting a lifetime of belongings. The relief they report is comfort and control, not "paradise." A made-up story is very likely lawful only when it starts with a visible "Example:" label and nothing on the page implies the people are clients. An invented story presented as real is a fake testimonial under the FTC's 2024 rule, with civil penalties of **up to $53,088 per violation** for knowing violations ([16 CFR 465.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-465.2); [16 CFR 1.98(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-1.98)). Under fair housing law, a story may describe the buyer's own needs but never who lives in an area ([24 CFR 100.75](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.75)). For search, real stories from the firm's files count as first-hand experience in Google's guidelines, and labelled examples only teach. Answer engines quote single sentences, so every local fact belongs outside the story.

## Stories raise recall moderately and intentions slightly

Grades in this report: **A** is a meta-analysis or a large pre-registered replication. **B** is a single study or one lab. **C** is a popular claim with a weak source or none. **X** is contested: a failed replication or a bias-corrected reanalysis cuts against it.

The strongest finding is about memory. A 2021 meta-analysis pooled 150 effect sizes from 33,078 participants. People **recalled stories better than expository text (g = .72)** and understood them better (g = .48) ([Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)). By a standard conversion (Cohen's U3), the typical story reader recalls more than about three in four readers of the plain version. That is a real advantage, not a 22-fold one. The limits matter. The studies varied a great deal (I² = 98%). Only four tested recall after a delay. Memory results showed some sign of publication bias. The authors warn against forcing all information into story form. Their comparisons are whole texts, so they do not show that a short story inside a factual page raises recall of the page's facts. **[A]**

Persuasion effects are smaller. In experiments that gave people a story instead of no story, the shifts were **r = .17 for beliefs, .19 for attitudes, .17 for intentions and .23 for behaviors** ([Braddock and Dillard 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555); figures from the abstract). In 25 health studies with 9,330 people, the overall effect was r = .063, and **printed stories showed no significant effect** while audio and video did ([Shen, Sheer and Li 2015](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/impact-of-narratives-on-persuasion-in-health-communication-a-meta/)). A blog story is printed text, so a writer should expect a modest effect from it. **[A]**

People respond to stories and numbers in different ways. One meta-analysis found that "statistical evidence was found to have a stronger influence than narrative evidence on beliefs and attitude, whereas narrative evidence had a stronger influence on intention" ([Zebregs et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/10410236.2013.842528)). Another found a single story did better than a non-story on attitudes and intentions, but not on beliefs ([Oschatz and Marker 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqaa017)). For a page, this means a plain split. Sourced sentences tell the reader what is true. The story helps the reader picture making the move. **[A]**

Readers who are absorbed in a story, which researchers call "transported," hold more story-consistent beliefs and rate the main character more favorably ([Green and Brock 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701)). A meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes found three things a writer controls that raise absorption: an **identifiable character (ρ = .20), a plot the reader can picture (ρ = .29) and lifelike detail (ρ = .27)** ([van Laer et al. 2014](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/)). The authors call these effects "small but not trivial." The same data showed **reader age had no link to absorption (ρ = .00, 34 samples)**. Similarity between reader and character helps a little, and similarity of situation and outlook helps more than matching age or other demographics ([Green and Appel 2024 review, p. 15](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf)). There is no evidence-based reason to write differently for a 70-year-old. Write for the reader's situation instead. A second meta-analysis found absorption was stronger for commercial stories than non-commercial ones, so a sales setting does not by itself cancel the effect ([van Laer et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2018.10.053)). **[A, small]**

A few single studies suggest how to build a short story. Claims placed where they change what happens next in a story were remembered better, persuaded more and were judged truer two weeks later ([Dahlstrom 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650210362683)). A cause-and-effect sequence, a goal, a conflict and a turning point all raise absorption, and attention narrows at the moments of most suspense ([Green and Appel 2024, pp. 7, 17](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf)). A character who doubts at first and then changes their mind persuaded more than one who was sure from the start (Huang and Green, in press, summarized in the same review, p. 38). Labelling the same story as fact or as fiction usually did not change how absorbed readers became, but a fact label led readers to look harder at weak arguments (Green et al. 2006, same review, pp. 30 to 31). That last point matters for this site. It suggests an "Example" label does not reduce absorption much, and that a story told as true gets checked harder. This is an extrapolation: those studies tested fact and fiction labels, not the word "Example." **[B]**

People give endings more importance when they look back. A meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes found that the most intense moment and the final moment of an experience predicted how people rated it afterward (**r = .58**), while its length had essentially no effect ([Alaybek et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149)). In a trial of 682 colonoscopy patients, adding a short, less painful ending made patients rate the whole procedure as less unpleasant ([Redelmeier, Katz and Kahneman 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0304-3959%2803%2900003-4)). When people judged another person's whole life, they gave its ending the most importance, and adding mildly pleasant years at the end made a wonderful life look worse ([Diener, Wirtz and Oishi 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00321)). These are studies of experiences and judged lives, not of reading short texts. "End on a clear outcome and stop" is a reasonable extension, not a tested rule for blog stories. **[A for experiences; untested for text]**

Concrete, easy-to-picture text was rated easier to understand and was recalled better across four experiments, but the evidence comes from one research group ([Sadoski, Goetz and Rodriguez 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.85)). "Imagine yourself" appeals did not work, and sometimes hurt, when the scene was hard to picture ([Petrova and Cialdini 2005](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i3p442-452.html)). A reader who has never seen Myrtle Beach needs one or two concrete details before any "imagine." The claim that concrete wording makes a statement seem truer failed a pre-registered replication ([Henderson, Vallée-Tourangeau and Simons 2019](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.192)). **[B; truth effect X]**

Stories also fail in known ways. When researchers made the selling intent of an ad obvious by adding rhetorical questions, "the relative advantage of narrative ads over expository ads disappears" ([Wentzel, Tomczak and Herrmann 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.20341)). A later meta-analysis found stories still met less resistance than non-stories on average (d = -.213), even when the persuasive intent was plain ([Ratcliff and Sun 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/hcr/hqz017); [Green and Appel 2024, p. 39](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf)). The safe reading is to keep rhetorical questions and the sales line out of the story itself. Details that did not fit the setting lowered realism and absorption (Busselle and Vierrether 2022, same review, pp. 42 to 43). Characters who model a behavior can raise readers' intention to do it, even when the writer did not intend that ([van Laer et al. 2014](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/)), so the writer should spend the story's sentences on the better choice, not on the mistake. **No study found tests stories of 5 to 10 sentences, or stories in home buying.** Everything in this section comes from studies of longer texts, ads and health messages.

### Seven popular claims fail a source check

The most quoted number in storytelling advice has no source. Stanford's women's leadership site quotes Jennifer Aaker: "Stories are remembered up to 22 times more than facts alone," with no study attached ([Stanford Women's Leadership](https://womensleadership.stanford.edu/resources/voice-influence/harnessing-power-stories)). Many pages credit Jerome Bruner. A writer who read Bruner's *Actual Minds, Possible Worlds* found "nothing whatsoever about stories being more memorable than facts" and found the figure only in one Aaker video ([Patience-Davies](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/just-where-did-claim-stories-22-times-more-memorable-patience-davies-ud7le/)). A second check reached the same answer ([Intela Learning](https://intelalearning.wordpress.com/2020/07/23/storytelling-works-doesnt-it/)).

The oxytocin claim comes from small studies in one lab. Paul Zak's essay reports that a story with a dramatic arc raised blood oxytocin, that oxytocin raised donations by 56 percent, and that a model predicts donation "with 82 percent accuracy," the last with no citation ([Zak 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4445577/)). The trust finding behind it ([Kosfeld et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature03701)) failed a registered replication with 677 men and more than 95% power ([Declerck et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0878-x)). A review found no "robust convergent evidence" that trust is caused by oxytocin ([Nave, Camerer and McCullough 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26581735/)). Common blood assays for oxytocin "lack reliability" on unextracted samples ([McCullough, Churchland and Mendez 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2013.04.018)). One analysis concluded "most of the published intranasal OT findings do not represent true effects" ([Walum, Waldman and Young 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2015.06.016)).

The identifiable victim effect is also weak. The classic study found a story about one girl, Rokia, drew more donations than statistics about millions ([Small, Loewenstein and Slovic 2007](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/increase-charitable-donations-appeal-heart-not-head)). A meta-analysis of 41 studies put the effect at **r = .05** ([Lee and Feeley 2016](https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/the-identifiable-victim-effect-a-meta-analytic-review/)). A 2024 pre-registered replication of the classic study "found no empirical support" for it (ηp² = .000), though it used hypothetical donations, not real money ([Maier, Wong and Feldman 2024](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.90203)). The research notes disagree here: the craft notes list this study as tested evidence that numbers weaken a story, while the science notes show it did not replicate. This report follows the science notes. Keep numbers out of the story because the owner asked for few numbers and because statistics changed beliefs more than stories did, not because of Rokia.

Three smaller cautions belong with this list. The "93% recall" often quoted from Bower and Clark (1969) comes from people who made up their own stories to memorize word lists, which says nothing about reading someone else's story ([Bower and Clark 1969](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03332778)). The "Pixar rules" are 22 tips one former storyboard artist, Emma Coats, posted on Twitter in 2011, not a studio rulebook ([RedShark News](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling)). Donald Miller's results for StoryBrand clients are self-reported marketing ([StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)).

## Every short-story method uses the same three moves

Each method in the craft research reduces to the same shape: show the normal, then one event that changes it, then what is different after. Kenn Adams' story spine is eight sentences with fixed openings: "once upon a time, every day, but one day, because of that, because of that, because of that, until finally, and ever since then" ([NPR interview, 2026](https://www.nhpr.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories)). It reached Pixar through an improv teacher who taught it to the writers (same source), and it is rule 4 of Emma Coats' tips ([RedShark News](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling)). Kindra Hall's slide deck uses three labels: the normal, the explosion and the new normal ([Hall slide deck](https://lockbox.lockton.com/asset/b293d2ba-fc9d-4d49-8b27-c68bdfce9535/Strategic-Storytelling-for-Impact.pdf)). Trey Parker of South Park adds one test: "What should happen between every beat that you've written down, is either the word 'therefore' or 'but'." Matt Stone: "this happened, and then this happened, and then this happens ... that's not a movie" ([NYU class transcript](https://speakola.com/arts/matt-stone-trey-parker-nyu-writing-class-2014)). All of this is expert opinion. The tested work above agrees with the part about cause and effect: a point that changes the next event is remembered better ([Dahlstrom 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650210362683)).

Matthew Dicks, a many-time Moth StorySLAM winner, builds each story backward from one small moment of change. "I always tell people to start with the end of their story first," he says, and the opening should be the opposite of that ending ([Art of Manliness interview](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/)). He prefers small moments because "people can't connect to those big moments," says "the shorter story wins," and allows cutting and compressing time but never adding events that did not happen (same source). That last rule matches this repo's rule for the story bank. Reader notes on his book describe two devices that fit a short story: the "Elephant," a problem the audience sees early, and the "Backpack," where the listener learns the plan before it is tested ([calv.info notes](https://calv.info/bookshelf/storyworthy)). These come from a reader's notes, not the book text.

Kindra Hall names four parts: an identifiable character, authentic emotion, a specific moment in time and place, and specific details. On details she says, "Don't make them work too hard," and often one detail is enough ([Social Media Examiner interview](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/creating-stories-that-stick-how-marketers-can-win-with-story-kindra-hall/)). One summary says the product being sold does not count as a character ([Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=101525)). Donald Miller makes the same point for brands: "Your brand is not the hero of the story. Your customer is." A guide in a story shows empathy and authority, and stakes matter because "if we don't know what failure might befall the hero, audiences zone out" ([StoryBrand PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf)). Miller describes three levels of problem: external, internal and philosophical (same source). For a retiree, the external problem is a house with stairs; the internal one is the worry about living there at 85. Miller's "failure" device needs care on a retiree page. A worst-case scene reads as selling through fear. One plain sentence about what the buyer wanted to avoid is enough.

Lisa Cron argues that readers follow how events change the main character inside: "A story is about how an unavoidable external problem forces the protagonist to change internally," and "you never have to tell us how they're feeling" ([Writer Unboxed, 2021](https://writerunboxed.com/2021/03/08/still-crazy-after-all-these-years/)). Flash writers agree on compression. Dinty Moore, editor of *Brevity*: "Every sentence has to do more than one thing" ([Lit Mag News](https://litmagnews.substack.com/p/well-make-it-short-nonfiction-a-chat)). Lee Martin points to "the turn at the end of an essay, the one where something rises that resonates" ([Talking Writing, 2020](https://mail.talkingwriting.com/talking-flash-nonfiction-brevity)). Journalists use a pairing that fits a blog well: a short anecdote, then a "nut graf" that "tells the reader what the writer is up to" ([Poynter, Chip Scanlan](https://poynter.org/?p=11371)). Scanlan names one failure that matters on long pages: a person introduced at the top who "is never seen or heard from again" (same source).

Chip and Dan Heath explain why experts write stories that fail. Once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it, which they call the curse of knowledge ([Harvard Business Review, 2006](https://hbr.org/2006/12/the-curse-of-knowledge)). Their example is Elizabeth Newton's 1990 study: people tapping well-known songs expected listeners to name them at least half the time, and listeners named 3 of 120 ([Wagonheim summary of the Heaths' account](https://wagonheim.com/the-tappers-and-the-listeners/)). This is why stories written by agents often include model names, zone codes and road numbers that a buyer cannot picture. The Heaths say a story gives "simulation (knowledge about how to act) and inspiration (motivation to act)" ([quoted in a University of Minnesota journal article](https://pubs.lib.umn.edu/index.php/jcotr/article/download/2700/2108/9491)). Of their three plots, the Connection plot (a gap closed between people) and the Challenge plot (a hard task made manageable) fit most retiree moves ([Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/storytelling-communication/)).

One conflict needs a ruling. The craft sources say end on an action or image and leave the meaning unstated. The owner's rule says "a story ends with what happened and what it means for the reader, in plain words" (`voice/RULES.md`, STORY-5). This report follows the owner's rule. Both can be met: the last sentence of the story is the concrete outcome, and the very next sentence, outside the story, says what it means for the reader. That is Scanlan's nut graf placed after the anecdote instead of before it.

No source tests the best length for a marketing story. The brief asks for 5 to 10 sentences. This repo's standard asks for 5 to 8 (`STANDARD.md`, T2), and the story spine is eight. This report uses 5 to 8. The table shows how the moves fit.

| Sentence | What it does | Source of the idea |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The buyer in their old life, with the worry in it. This is the opposite of the ending. | Dicks; Hall's "normal"; Adams' "every day" |
| 2 | What they would lose, or what they want. | Coats rule 16 (stakes); Miller |
| 3 | "But": the event or trip that forces a choice. | Adams' "but one day"; Parker and Stone |
| 4 to 5 | What they did because of it. One detail the reader can picture. The company once at most, as a helper. | Adams' "because of that"; Hall; Miller |
| 6 to 7 | The small moment of change, then the concrete outcome. | Dicks; Martin; peak-end research |
| After | One plain sentence on what this means for the reader. | Scanlan; owner rule STORY-5 |

## Retirees worry most about money, doctors, family and friends

Two different readers arrive at these pages. Boomers were **42% of all home buyers** in the year to June 2024, and buyers over 60 moved a **median of only 35 miles** ([NAR 2025 Generational Trends](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)). The retiree moving to Myrtle Beach from New York or Ohio is in the long-distance minority. That reader asks what the area is like, where the doctors are and how to buy from far away. The Myrtle Beach metro had the **fastest-growing 65-and-over population of any US metro, up 23.1% from 2020 to 2023, to 107,430** ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2024/06/metro-areas-population-age.html)), and AP reported another 6.3% in 2024 ([AP via Insurance Journal](https://amp.insurancejournal.com/news/southeast/2025/07/02/830025.htm)). People moving into Horry County from other states came most often from **North Carolina, New York, Pennsylvania and Florida**, with Ohio eighth, in 2018 to 2022 Census flows for all ages ([Census ACS county flows](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/demo/tables/geographic-mobility/2022/state-to-county-migration-2018-2022/state-to-county-migration-flows/StxCty_US_ACS_2018-2022.txt)). U-Haul's 2026 data, reported by Fortune, ranked South Carolina first and North Carolina second for boomer moves, with Myrtle Beach second among metros; it counts U-Haul customers only ([Fortune, 2026-10-07](https://fortune.com/2026/10/07/baby-boomers-moving-carolinas-florida-uhaul-retirement/)). The city's longtime spokesman told AP that vacationers from places like Ohio and New York realized they could retire early: "The climate's great, taxes are low, there's a wealth of opportunities for recreation, dining and shopping" ([AP via Insurance Journal](https://amp.insurancejournal.com/news/southeast/2025/07/02/830025.htm)).

Family is the strongest reason to buy at this age. **Being closer to family and friends was the top reason for buyers 60 to 69 (19%) and 70 to 78 (29%)**. A smaller home was the main reason for 10% and 18%. Better weather was 2% ([NAR 2025, Exhibit 1-15](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)). Among retirees who did move, lower cost of living (37%) and being closer to family (36%) led, and 16% moved to another state ([CNBC/SurveyMonkey 2024](https://www.surveymonkey.com/curiosity/cnbc-retirement-2024/)). For a northern retiree moving south, family is often a reason not to go, because the move takes them away from grandchildren. Del Webb's 2023 survey of Midwest adults considering a move ran under the headline "Trading the Beach for Grandbabies?" and found 62% planned to stay in the same general area ([Del Webb press release](https://s204.q4cdn.com/680895981/files/doc_news/2023/08/1/8-17-23-trading-the-beach-for-grandbabies-del-webb-building-new-55-communities-to-satisfy-growing-demand-for-retirees-in.pdf)). A story can show that conflict honestly.

Retirees often say they are confident about money but act worried. **78% of retirees say they are confident they will have enough, yet nearly half spend less than they could because they worry about running out**. Health costs were higher than expected for 38%, and housing for 35% ([EBRI/Greenwald 2025 Retirement Confidence Survey](https://greenwaldresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2025-rcs-release-report.pdf)). In another survey, 56% of retirees were concerned about running out of savings ([CNBC/SurveyMonkey 2024](https://www.surveymonkey.com/curiosity/cnbc-retirement-2024/)). Among adults 50 and over who expect to move, the reasons AARP reports are rising rent or mortgage costs (71%), upkeep (60%) and taxes (55%); the release does not state the base for these three figures ([AARP 2024](https://www.aarp.org/press/releases/2024-12-10-new-aarp-report-majority-adults-50-plus-age-place-policies-communities-catch-up.html)). The right story moment is the second check of the budget, not panic.

Health care matters more with each decade. **35% of buyers 70 to 78 chose their neighborhood partly to be near health facilities, against 19% of all buyers** ([NAR 2025, Exhibit 2-7](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)). A gerontologist who studies these choices put it this way: "A dwelling not just a place to live. It's a place to manage your health" ([KU News, 2017](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2017/09/13/aging-populations-often-thoroughly-contemplate-housing-decisions-researchers-find)). A 72-year-old reader is likely to ask how far the hospital is before asking about the pool.

Loneliness after a long move tends to be social, not emotional. In a study of 4,995 Dutch retirees who moved abroad, movers were socially lonelier than those who stayed, but not emotionally lonelier. Contact with neighbors and a sense of belonging lowered both kinds ([Euronews summary of Savaş et al. 2025](https://www.euronews.com/health/2025/03/13/thinking-of-retiring-abroad-you-might-be-lonelier-than-if-you-stayed-home-new-study-sugges)). This was read only as a news summary. A neighbor who knocks is the kind of contact that helped. Sorting belongings is one of the hardest tasks. More than 100 people interviewed about downsizing called it "one of the hardest things they've ever done," families often wonder why it was not started sooner, and people keep things for "possible selves" ([KU News, 2020](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2020/05/18/downsizing-book-encourages-older-people-confront-their-possessions)).

Storms and insurance are the costs a northern buyer may not expect. In Horry County, the state's coastal wind area is the land east of U.S. 17 or Bypass 17, whichever is farther west ([S.C. Code 38-75-310](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t38c075.php)). Hurricane Florence caused major flooding in Horry and Georgetown counties in 2018 ([USGS](https://water.usgs.gov/alerts/2018/item_2018-09-25_19:41:05.html)). Online premium estimates for Myrtle Beach disagree widely and come from lead-generation sites, so they should not be used. This site never states a premium or a payment amount (`CLAUDE.md`). A story can show the question and the preparation without a number.

Delay is normal, and stories should not shame it. People aged 80 to 89 in Sweden and Germany often put off the decision, and one said, "I feel lonely, and that is why I think I should move, but I don't want to really" ([Löfqvist et al. 2013](https://lucris.lub.lu.se/ws/files/1369832/4022872.pdf)). That sample is older than these readers. US retirees aged 67 to 97 "are continually thinking about, 'Where is the best place that I should grow old?'" ([KU News, 2017](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2017/09/13/aging-populations-often-thoroughly-contemplate-housing-decisions-researchers-find)). Most buyers 60 to 69 said it was "just the right time" (47%), and 17% said they had little choice ([NAR 2025, Exhibit 1-16](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)).

Relief, in the research, means comfort and control. A gerontology model calls the goal "residential normalcy": a home that feels comfortable and one the person can manage ([Hasselt University paper on Golant's model](https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/bitstream/1942/46092/1/Residential%20Normalcy%20in%20Later%20Life%20%20A%20Life%20Course%20Perspective.pdf); read as a search summary). People who finished downsizing reported feeling empowered, according to a search summary of Ekerdt's book ([Columbia University Press blog](https://cupblog.org/?p=43585)). For buyers over 60 who found a step of buying hard, the hardest was **finding the right property (55%)**. **Help finding the right home was what 55% to 57% wanted most from an agent**, far ahead of help negotiating (10% to 13%) ([NAR 2025, Exhibits 3-5 and 4-2](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)). A story about how the right house was chosen fits what these buyers say they want. A story that ends on "paradise" does not fit the evidence.

## A made-up story needs a visible "Example" label

Under federal rules, a customer story is an endorsement when readers are likely to believe it reflects the experience of someone other than the advertiser, and "product" includes a service, brand or company ([16 CFR 255.0(b), (d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)). The FTC's own example says a scene with "unidentified shoppers" is an "obviously fictional dramatization" and "would not be an endorsement" ([16 CFR 255.0(g)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)). An actor reading a first-person script about personal results is still an endorsement (255.0(g)(6), same page). That is a reason to avoid first-person examples. A meta-analysis found first-person stories raised identification a little but not absorption ([Green and Appel 2024, pp. 15 to 16](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf)), and that small gain is not worth the legal risk. When an ad is aimed at older consumers, the FTC judges a disclosure "from the perspective of older consumers, including those with diminished auditory, visual, or cognitive processing abilities." Online, a disclosure "should be unavoidable" and must not be "contradicted or mitigated by, or inconsistent with, anything else in the communication" ([16 CFR 255.0(f), (g)(10)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0)). A small grey footnote is weak under that test. A bold "Example:" at the start of the story, in body-size text, meets it.

The 2024 testimonial rule prohibits a business from writing or creating a testimonial that materially misrepresents "that the reviewer or testimonialist exists," that they used the service, or "the reviewer's or testimonialist's experience" ([16 CFR 465.2(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-465.2)). The rule took effect October 21, 2024 ([Morgan Lewis summary](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/08/ftc-issues-final-rule-on-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials)). Asked about clearly dramatized ads, the FTC said "it would not be reasonable for viewers to consider 'an obviously fictional dramatization' to be an endorsement," and it limited the rule to material misrepresentations ([89 FR 68034 preamble](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm)). A clearly labelled example is therefore very likely outside the rule, but no rule text exempts it by name. The maximum civil penalty is **$53,088 per violation** ([16 CFR 1.98(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-1.98)), unchanged for 2026 ([FTC notice, 2026-09-15](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-18853.pdf)). Penalties require proof that the business knew the practice was deceptive and prohibited (same preamble). A business that invents a client story knows it is invented. This is why the repo's standard keeps the label even though the owner asked, on 2026-10-07, for examples written "as if it was a real thing that chapter3 did" (`STANDARD.md`, T2). Removing the label turns a lawful example into a fake testimonial.

Real stories have their own limits. They must reflect the real experience, may not be "reworded so as to distort" it, and must use the person's exact words wherever quotation marks appear ([16 CFR 255.1(a), (b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.1)). Adding one detail to a real story can misrepresent the experience under the testimonial rule, which matches the repo rule to never add a detail. A real story that reports a key result is read as typical, so the page should state "the generally expected performance in the depicted circumstances" ([16 CFR 255.2(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.2)). The FTC tested disclaimers like "Results not typical" and found that "neither disclosure adequately reduced the communication that the experiences depicted are generally representative" ([16 CFR 255.2(e), Example 2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.2)). **This matters for the site's own practice.** The story bank's New York tax story shipped with the line "not a promise" (`stories/stories.json`, ny-client-tax-drop), and the owner's rule asks for "his numbers, not a promise" after one specific outcome (STORY-5). That line is close to the wording the FTC found insufficient on its own. Next to a money result, add the generally expected result from a verified fact row. A relationship the reader would not expect, such as a relative or employee telling the story, must be disclosed ([16 CFR 255.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.5)). Every number inside an example is the firm's own claim and needs a source ([16 CFR 255.1(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.1)). If an example shows the firm doing something, the firm must really do it.

South Carolina has no statute that names testimonials. Its license law allows discipline for "a substantial misrepresentation," "false promises likely to influence, persuade, or induce," a "continued and flagrant course of misrepresentation" in advertising, and guaranteeing "future profits from the resale of real estate" ([S.C. Code 40-57-710(A)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c057.php)). A story that implies a home will gain value is a risk under the last of these. Every ad must show the full brokerage name, and a trade name must reveal its holder ([S.C. Code 40-57-135(E)(2), (3)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t40c057.php)). A licensee may not advertise another firm's listing, or use its photos, without written permission (40-57-135(E)(1), same page), and the Commission issued a reminder in June 2025 after more complaints ([SC LLR reminder](https://llr.sc.gov/re/News/Reminder%20of%20Advertising%20Restrictions.pdf)). New advertising regulations, Regulation Document 5437, sat in a Senate committee as of the last check, so their status is unknown ([S. 1073](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/sess126_2025-2026/bills/1073.htm)). For REALTOR members, the NAR Code requires "a true picture" in advertising, limits "sold" claims to brokers in the deal, and prohibits "misleading images" ([NAR 2026 Code of Ethics, Article 12](https://www.nar.realtor/about-nar/governing-documents/code-of-ethics/2026-code-of-ethics-standards-of-practice)). Whether Chapter3's agents are members was not checked.

Under fair housing law, the risk is in what a story says about who lives somewhere, not in what it says about the buyer's own needs. Any statement about selling a dwelling may not indicate "any preference, limitation, or discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin," and this includes "words, phrases, photographs, illustrations" ([24 CFR 100.75](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.75)). South Carolina's law has the same list ([S.C. Code 31-21-40(3)](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t31c021.php)). Age itself is not on either list. A qualified 55-and-over community must have at least one person 55 or older in at least 80% of occupied units ([24 CFR 100.305](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.305)). Phrases like "adult living" or "adult community" in its ads "are not consistent" with that status ([24 CFR 100.306(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/section-100.306)). A fair housing handout lists words that "could be discriminatory," including "retirees," "empty nesters," "mature couple," "healthy only" and "able-bodied," and lists "Quiet neighborhood," "Swimming pool" and "Golf course, nearby" as acceptable ([City of Spokane handout](https://static.spokanecity.org/documents/blog/2015/04/06/register-for-the-2015-inland-northwest-fair-housing-conference/advertising-and-the-internet.pdf); secondary). HUD withdrew some advertising guidance in 2025, but its notice says actions that do not comply with the Act "continue to be subject to enforcement" ([FR Doc. 2026-06624](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-06624.pdf)). The story bank already has a real case of this risk. The owner's account of the biggest mistake New York buyers make was first told as matching neighbors by type. It was marked "NOT PUBLISHABLE, steering," and shipped only as "check that the community's rules fit how you live" (`stories/stories.json`, ny-buyer-rules-fit). No source addresses composite stories that blend several clients. A composite is not one person's experience, so it should be labelled "Example."

| A story may say | A story may not say or imply |
|---|---|
| The example people's names, ages and the state they come from ("from Ohio") | The race, color, religion, sex, disability, family status or national origin of anyone who lives in an area |
| The buyer's own family and reasons ("their grandchildren in Pennsylvania") | Who a home or area suits: "perfect for empty nesters," "active adults," "people like you," "no kids around" |
| A qualified community's real age rule, from a verified fact row | "Adult community" or "adult living" for a 55-and-over community |
| Features and places: one floor, a spare bedroom, a porch, golf nearby | A promise of savings, value gain or a storm-free life |
| What a Chapter3 agent does, where the story bank records it | "Our client," "we helped," "Chapter3 found them," or a quote nobody said |

## Real stories show Google experience; examples only teach

Google's rater guidelines ask raters to "consider the extent to which the content creator has the necessary first-hand or life experience for the topic." They rate pages with "false testimonials" and "'fake' owner or content creator profiles" as Lowest ([Search Quality Evaluator Guidelines, sections 3.4 and 4.5.3](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/guidelines.raterhub.com/en//searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf)). For money and life topics, "pages that share first-hand life experience" can rate high when the content is trustworthy, but some advice "must come from experts" (section 3.4.1, same PDF). Google's helpful content page, updated 2026-10-05, asks whether content shows "first-hand expertise" and says "trust is most important" ([Google helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)). Its AI optimization guide says "a first-hand review provides a unique perspective based on personal experience" ([Google AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). A real story from the firm's files, told with exact, checkable details, is the strongest kind of story for search. A labelled example can make a page more helpful, but Google does not treat it as experience and says nothing about it either way. An unlabelled invented story is worse than none on both counts: a fake testimonial under the FTC rule and a Lowest signal for raters.

Publishing many story pages has its own risk. Google's spam policy defines scaled content abuse as "many pages ... generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users ... no matter how it's created," including using AI "to generate many pages without adding value" ([Google spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)). Google also uses "some site-wide signals" ([Google Search Central, March 2024](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2024/03/core-update-spam-policies)). Twenty relocation pages that share one example with the name and state swapped would match that pattern. Each story should be specific to its page's question. Google asks site owners to "manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content" before publishing ([Google on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content)).

Answer engines quote single sentences, so facts need sentences of their own. Google says there are "no additional requirements" to appear in AI Overviews. Its systems may issue "multiple related searches across subtopics." Site owners can use `nosnippet` or `data-nosnippet` to keep a block out of snippets and AI features ([Google AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)). Microsoft says AI assistants "break content into smaller, usable pieces," can "lift a single line," and favor "sentences that make sense even when pulled out of context" ([Microsoft Advertising, October 2025](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/october-2025/optimizing-your-content-for-inclusion-in-ai-search-answers)). A study of generative engines found that adding quotations, statistics and citations raised visibility; storytelling was not tested ([Aggarwal et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735)). An analysis of 1.2 million ChatGPT answers found 44.2% of citations came from the first 30% of a page ([Search Engine Land, secondary](https://searchengineland.com/chatgpt-citations-content-study-469483)). **No source found says whether answer engines quote stories, or how they treat a labelled example.** Four practices follow. The answer goes at the top of the page; the story goes below it. A sourced local fact goes in its own plain sentence outside the story, so an engine quotes the fact and not the character. The "Example:" label appears once, so a later sentence quoted alone loses it. Every story sentence should be harmless on its own. A sentence that gives an example person's tax bill, quoted alone, reads as a real case. A sentence in which Linda times the drive to the emergency room does not. Use `data-nosnippet` only on an example whose sentences cannot stand alone, because it also stops the block from being cited.

## The playbook

Use a real story first. The story bank (`stories/stories.json`) has 30 stories. Real stories are the strongest material for readers, for search and under the law. Tell each one as recorded, with no added detail, and about "an agent at Chapter3," never with the company as the actor (`voice/RULES.md`, STORY-1). The owner's notes recorded no state-specific client stories as of August 2026 (`stories/stories.json`, client-origins). Where no real story fits the page's question, write an example under the rules below.

### A checklist for any 5 to 10 sentence story

| # | Check | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The story starts with **Example:** in bold, body-size text. A story-bank story is told as recorded instead. | [16 CFR 255.0(f)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/section-255.0); [465 preamble](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm); T2 |

| 3 | 5 to 8 sentences (the repo standard; the brief allows up to 10). Each has 28 words or fewer. | T2; `voice/RULES.md` rule 3 |
| 4 | One buyer or one couple, first names only. | [van Laer 2014](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/); [Hall](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/creating-stories-that-stick-how-marketers-can-win-with-story-kindra-hall/) |

### Eight examples, weak and strong

Each pair below is labelled "Example." None claims to be a client or a real event. Family members appear only as the buyer's own family; no example says who lives in an area or who a home suits. No story has more than three numbers, and every number is about the people, not the market. The weak versions fail on craft, not on the law, so the contrast is about writing. Each strong version is followed by the line that goes after the story and a short note on what changed.

#### Example 1: Leaving the grandchildren

Being closer to family was the top reason to buy for people 60 to 78 ([NAR 2025](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)). For a northern couple, a move south often means moving away from grandchildren.

**Weak.**

> **Example:** Many retirees worry about leaving their family when they move south. Tom and Diane felt the same way. They were nervous, but in the end they knew it was the right decision. Now they love their new life at the beach, and the family visits all the time. Do not let worry hold you back from the life you deserve!

**Strong.**

> **Example:** For twelve years, Tom and Diane lived ten minutes from their grandchildren in Pennsylvania. Moving to the beach meant the end of Sunday dinners at their house. But they had wanted to retire near the ocean for most of their lives. They decided the new house had to be a place the family would want to visit. They passed on a house with a bigger yard and chose one with a spare bedroom and a pull-out couch. Before they left Pennsylvania, Diane wrote the grandchildren's first visit on the new kitchen calendar.

**Line after the story:** If you want family to visit, look for a place for them to sleep before you look at the yard.

**What changed.** The weak version is a summary with named feelings and an idiom ("hold you back"), and it ends on a sales line, which makes the selling intent plain ([Wentzel 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.20341)). The strong version opens on the loss, uses "but" for the turn, shows the decision as a choice between two houses, and ends on one object the reader can picture. It follows the Connection plot ([Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/storytelling-communication/)).

#### Example 2: Will the money last

78% of retirees feel confident about money, yet nearly half spend less than they could because they worry about running out ([EBRI 2025](https://greenwaldresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2025-rcs-release-report.pdf)).

**Weak.**

> **Example:** Retirement is expensive, and money is a big worry for a lot of people. Marcus was worried his savings would not last. After looking at many homes, he found one that fit his budget perfectly. Now he has total peace of mind. The beach is waiting for you too!

**Strong.**

> **Example:** Marcus paid off his house in Ohio the year before he retired, and he did not want a house payment again. He checked his savings account every Friday, even in good months. But the first houses he toured near the beach cost almost everything he got for his Ohio house. He wrote one number on an index card: the most he would spend, with money left over for a new roof someday. He did not make an offer on a house with an ocean view, because its price was over the number on the card. He bought a smaller house a short drive from the beach, and the card is still in his wallet.

**Line after the story:** Pick the most you will spend before you tour, and keep money aside for repairs.

**What changed.** The weak version promises "total peace of mind" and gives the beach a human action. The strong version shows the quiet worry as a habit (checking savings every Friday), states the plan before it is tested (Dicks's "Backpack," [calv.info notes](https://calv.info/bookshelf/storyworthy)), tests it with an ocean view, and ends on the card. It names no price, rate or payment amount.

#### Example 3: Knowing no one

Retirees who moved far were socially lonelier, and contact with neighbors lowered loneliness ([Euronews on Savaş et al. 2025](https://www.euronews.com/health/2025/03/13/thinking-of-retiring-abroad-you-might-be-lonelier-than-if-you-stayed-home-new-study-sugges)).

**Weak.**

> **Example:** Joan was scared she would be lonely after leaving New York. She did not know a single person in South Carolina. Luckily, she joined some clubs and made lots of new friends. Now she says moving was the best decision of her life. Follow your dreams!

**Strong.**

> **Example:** Joan lived on the same block in Queens for 30 years and knew every neighbor by name. In South Carolina she would know no one. On her house-hunting trip, she toured homes in the late afternoon, when people were outside. On one street, a neighbor walking a dog stopped to say hello and told her which day the trash is picked up. Joan made an offer on that street the next morning. In her first week in the new house, the same neighbor knocked and asked her to lunch on Thursday.

**Line after the story:** Tour at the hour you would usually be home, so you see the street on a normal day.

**What changed.** In the weak version, luck solves the problem ([Coats rule 19](https://www.redsharknews.com/production/item/509-pixar-s-rules-for-great-storytelling)), and "now she says" reads like a testimonial. The strong version makes the opening the opposite of the ending (every neighbor known, then no one, then a knock), and the buyer's own choice of when to tour makes the meeting possible. It describes no one on the street by any trait.

#### Example 4: Where is the hospital

35% of buyers 70 to 78 chose their neighborhood partly for health care, against 19% of all buyers ([NAR 2025](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)).

**Weak.**

> **Example:** Ray and Linda wanted to be sure they would have good health care in retirement. They did their homework and found that the area had everything they needed. They bought a lovely home and feel safe knowing help is close by. Why wait to start your new life?

**Strong.**

> **Example:** Ray was 72, and he wanted to know where the hospital was before he looked at a single pool. In North Carolina, he and Linda had seen the same doctors for years. On their house-hunting trip, Linda drove from each house they liked to the nearest emergency room and timed the drive. They crossed one house off their list because the drive took too long. Before they made an offer on the next house, Ray called two doctors' offices to ask if they took new patients. On the day they closed, his first appointment was already on the calendar.

**Line after the story:** Before you make an offer, drive from the house to the hospital, and call a doctor's office to ask about new patients.

**What changed.** The weak version uses an idiom ("did their homework"), makes a vague claim about the area, names a feeling and ends on a rhetorical question. The strong version puts this reader's real priority in the first sentence, shows two checks the reader can copy, and ends on proof of control. It names no health condition and makes no claim about local doctors.

#### Example 5: Forty years of belongings

People who downsized called it "one of the hardest things they've ever done" ([KU News, 2020](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2020/05/18/downsizing-book-encourages-older-people-confront-their-possessions)).

**Weak.**

> **Example:** Downsizing is one of the hardest parts of moving. Rosa had a lot of stuff after 40 years in her house. It was very emotional, but she got through it, and now she enjoys a simpler life. Downsizing is the first step toward the retirement you deserve.

**Strong.**

> **Example:** Rosa lived in the same house outside Charlotte for 40 years, and the basement was full. The house she wanted near the beach had no basement. For two months she sorted one shelf each morning. Her son wanted her to keep the dining table, but she chose her mother's sewing box instead. She sold the table to a neighbor and let her son take the good dishes. On her first night in the new house, she put the sewing box on the bedroom dresser.

**Line after the story:** Start sorting months before you list your house, and decide early which few things go with you.

**What changed.** The weak version is a summary that ends on a figure of speech ("the first step toward") and a sales line. The strong version gives one hard limit (no basement), a slow routine, a family disagreement, and a choice that shows what she values. The last sentence is an act with one object in it.

#### Example 6: Storms and the insurance question

In Horry County, the coastal wind area is written into state law ([S.C. Code 38-75-310](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t38c075.php)), and the story bank records that Chapter3 can get a buyer an insurance quote before they make an offer (`stories/stories.json`, insurance-quote-before-offer). This example uses the present tense, as the standard asks when a story shows a Chapter3 service.

**Weak.**

> **Example:** Mike and Sue were terrified of hurricanes. Everyone told them coastal insurance was a nightmare. But they found a great policy, and now they never worry about storms. Do not let a little wind keep you from paradise!

**Strong.**

> **Example:** Mike and Sue are leaving Long Island, and every friend asks them about hurricanes. They like a house a few streets from the water. But they do not know what it will cost to insure, and they do not want to find out after they sign. Before they make an offer, a Chapter3 agent gets them an insurance quote for that address. They compare it with the quote on a house farther inland, and they choose with both numbers in front of them. Sue writes a storm list and tapes it inside the pantry door: papers, medicine, and where they will go. When the first storm warning comes that fall, Mike takes the list down and starts at the top.

**Line after the story:** Get an insurance quote on the exact house before you make an offer.

**What changed.** The weak version uses fear words and figures of speech, and "never worry about storms" promises a result no one can promise. The strong version names the real question (the cost to insure), shows the company once doing a recorded service, and ends on preparation instead of a claim that storms do no harm. It states no premium.

#### Example 7: Stairs at 85

US retirees keep asking where they should grow old ([KU News, 2017](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2017/09/13/aging-populations-often-thoroughly-contemplate-housing-decisions-researchers-find)), and 18% of buyers 70 to 78 bought mainly to get a smaller home ([NAR 2025](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)).

**Weak.**

> **Example:** A big house is a lot of work. Don and Ruth knew it was time to downsize. They bought a beautiful one-story home and absolutely love it. Now they finally have time to enjoy life to the fullest.

**Strong.**

> **Example:** Don and Ruth's house in Pennsylvania had three floors and a lawn that took Don all of Saturday to mow. They could still manage it. But every winter they asked each other if they would want those stairs at 85. Before their trip south, they agreed to look only at homes with the bedroom and the washer on the main floor. Ruth almost broke that rule for a house with an upstairs porch and a view of the marsh. They bought the one-story house next door instead. The first Saturday after the move, Don mowed the small front lawn before breakfast and went fishing.

**Line after the story:** Decide what you will need in your 80s before you tour, and keep to that list when you see a house you love.

**What changed.** The weak version has no stakes and ends on an idiom. The strong version states a rule, tests it with a house Ruth wanted, and keeps it, which is the doubter-then-decider pattern ([Green and Appel 2024, p. 38](https://mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/2024/Pdfs/Green___Appel__2024__Advances_Preprint.pdf)). The before and after are the same task: all of Saturday, then before breakfast. It plans for later years without naming any health condition.

#### Example 8: Ten years of "someday"

In a study of people 80 to 89, many put off a late-life move for years ([Löfqvist et al. 2013](https://lucris.lub.lu.se/ws/files/1369832/4022872.pdf)), and 47% of buyers 60 to 69 said they bought when it was "just the right time" ([NAR 2025](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-04/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-04-01-2025.pdf)).

**Weak.**

> **Example:** Frank and Pat had talked about moving to the beach for years. Finally, they decided it was time. They were sad to leave Ohio, but they knew it was the right choice. Today they are happier than ever. If you have been putting it off, now is the time to make your move!

**Strong.**

> **Example:** For ten years, Frank and Pat ended every beach vacation by saying they would move down someday. Then Frank retired, and that February he shoveled the driveway four times in a week. Pat still did not want to give up her Tuesday book club. On their next trip, she looked up book clubs before she looked at houses. She found one that met at a library ten minutes from a house they both liked. They listed the Ohio house that spring. On their first Tuesday in the new house, Pat went to book club.

**Line after the story:** If you have put off the move for years, write down the one thing you would miss most. Look for it first on your next trip.

**What changed.** The weak version pushes the reader with urgency and a superlative. The strong version treats the delay as normal, uses one small trigger, gives the doubt to one person, and lets her own act answer it. The last sentence resolves the exact stake raised in the third, so no person or worry is dropped ([Scanlan](https://poynter.org/?p=11371)).

## Conclusion

The legal limits and the evidence agree, so the writer's job is simpler than it looks. The law requires the "Example" label, and in studies a fact or fiction label did not change how absorbed readers became, so the label is unlikely to make readers less absorbed. Fair housing law forbids describing who lives somewhere, and the research shows a shared situation matters more than shared demographics, with reader age showing no link at all. A story written about the reader's own worry, with no description of anyone else, is both the lawful story and the one the evidence favors. For search, the same split applies: facts go in plain, self-contained sentences for beliefs and for answer engines, and the story is for the decision.

The larger gain is in more real stories, not better examples. Readers were more absorbed by stories from ordinary people than by professional ones ([van Laer et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2018.10.053)), Google rewards first-hand experience, and a true story stays accurate when readers check it more closely. The story bank had no state-specific client stories in August 2026. One habit would change that. After each retiree closing, the agent writes down the one small moment that buyer would tell a friend. Matthew Dicks does the same each day in what he calls "Homework for Life" ([Ribbonfarm](https://ribbonfarm.com/2021/06/24/storytelling-matthew-dicks/)). Exact words are kept only with the client's permission. No study has tested stories this short, or stories in home buying. The site's own results, such as contact requests on a page before and after a story is added, are the only test available for this audience.
