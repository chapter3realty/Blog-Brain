# Lead with the answer in large type

The best page for chapter3realty.com puts a plain answer and a short list of key facts in the first phone screen, sets body text at 18 pixels or more in near-black on a light background, keeps every section open and visible, and uses pictures only when they show a fact. That one design serves all three goals. Readers aged 65 and older succeeded on **55% of web tasks against 75% for adults 21 to 55, and took 43% longer** ([NN/g 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)). Their named barriers are small type, faint text, small tap targets and moving or hidden content ([NN/g 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/)). Most visitors read about **20% of the words on a page** ([NN/g 2008](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)) and spend **74% of their viewing time in the first two screens** ([NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). Google said in 2026 that pages need **no special design for AI Overviews**. The same index, ranking and snippet rules apply, and the content that matters most is first-hand and local, not a generic tips list ([Google AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). Microsoft says Copilot quotes short, self-contained answers under clear headings and may skip content hidden in tabs or accordions ([Microsoft, 2025](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/october-2025/optimizing-your-content-for-inclusion-in-ai-search-answers)). Two things changed in 2026: **Google stopped showing FAQ rich results on 7 May 2026**, and Google published a guide that tells sites not to split pages into small pieces for AI ([Google updates log](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)). The repo's rules already match most of this evidence. The largest gaps are on the website itself: body text is about 15.5 pixels, lines run about 90 characters, link color fails contrast, no article has a table of contents, and eight pages hide FAQ answers in closed accordions.

## Eight principles, ranked by the strength of their evidence

Each principle below carries a grade. **Tested** means a controlled study or measured data with a stated sample. **Standard** means a W3C rule or a platform's own documentation about its own system. **Observed** means a usability or eyetracking observation without statistics. **Expert** means guidance with no study behind it on the page. **Vendor** means a single test with unpublished methods.

| Rank | Principle | Grade | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Put the answer first. Write short, scannable, objective text. | Tested | Concise, scannable and objective text together made a site **124% more usable**; task time fell from 359 to 149 seconds ([Morkes and Nielsen 1997](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)). 57% of viewing time is above the fold ([NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). |
| 2 | Make text large, dark and well spaced. | Tested and Standard | Seniors were 43% slower ([NN/g 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)). Older adults read better with dark text on a light background ([NN/g, dark mode](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)). WCAG sets 4.5:1 contrast, 200% resize and 1.5 line height ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). |
| 3 | Hide nothing that matters. Move nothing on a timer. | Tested (all ages) and Standard | Hidden navigation cut content discovery by more than 20% ([NN/g 2016](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)). A rotating carousel hid an offer 80% of the time ([NN/g 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/auto-forwarding/)). Google's "Read more" links need visible text ([Google snippets](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/snippet)). |
| 4 | Publish first-hand local facts, with sources a reader can check. | Standard and Tested | Google names original, non-commodity content as the main factor for AI search ([Google AI guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). Adding sources, quotations and statistics raised visibility in AI answers by **over 40%** in a lab test ([Aggarwal et al., KDD 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735)). |
| 5 | Make CTAs look like content, and use few of them. | Tested | Users ignored a colored box placed between blocks of text ([NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/)). Objective text beat promotional text by 27% ([Morkes and Nielsen 1997](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)). |
| 6 | Use pictures that inform. Drop decorative ones. | Observed | Users studied informational images and ignored stock "filler" ([NN/g 2010](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)). Decorative images lengthen pages on phones ([NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/formatting-long-form-content/)). |
| 7 | Load fast on a phone. | Standard | Core Web Vitals are used in ranking, but relevance comes first ([Google page experience](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/page-experience)). |
| 8 | Add structured data that matches the visible page. | Standard | It helps rich results, but Google says no special markup is needed for AI features ([Google AI guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). |

Principle 1 has the strongest evidence and the widest reach. It helps older readers, younger scanners, Google's snippets and AI answers at the same time. Principle 8 has the weakest effect. It is cheap, so the site keeps it, but it is not where effort should go.

## Older readers need larger, darker and steadier pages

Most of this audience reads on a phone. **90% of adults aged 50 and older owned a smartphone in 2025**, up from 55% in 2016 ([AARP 2026 Tech Trends](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/2026-technology-trends-older-adults/)). Among adults 65 and older, 78% own one, and **17% use a smartphone as their only way online** ([Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/)). Adults aged 70 to 79 own tablets at higher rates than adults 50 to 69 ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/2026-technology-trends-older-adults/)). Three in five adults 50 and older say technology is not designed with their age in mind. A page must therefore work fully at phone width and on a tablet. It must also work with the phone's large-text setting turned on. A Zillow designer reported that **about 30% of Zillow app users had larger accessibility text sizes on**, and the old design cut off their text ([Knecht case study](https://www.mpknecht.com/zillow-home-detail-page-redesign)).

**Type size has a floor and a ceiling.** NN/g's senior studies set at least 12 point, which is 16 CSS pixels, as the minimum for sites aimed at older users ([NN/g 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)). A 2022 review of 12 studies found older adults prefer larger sizes on phones, with recommendations of 17 to 20 pixels for long reading. The same review found that very large print also slows reading ([Hou et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9376262/)). An eyetracking study found better comprehension at 18 point and above, but its readers were aged 14 to 54 and the test was on a desktop. By the standard conversion, 18 point is about 24 pixels ([Rello et al. 2016, summary](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/?p=10398)). The combined evidence supports **18 pixels for body text on phones and 19 to 20 pixels on desktop, and not above about 24 pixels**. The exact best size for readers aged 55 to 75 is not settled. The website today sets article paragraphs at 0.97rem, about 15.5 pixels, on every screen (`main p` in `assets/app.*.css`, website clone of 2026-10-01). That is below NN/g's 16-pixel floor.

**Contrast matters more than typeface.** Younger and older adults both did better on reading tasks with dark text on a light background ([NN/g, dark mode](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)). Faint, light-colored text was a named barrier in NN/g's latest senior tests ([NN/g 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/)). Studies of serif against sans serif type gave mixed results, and reading time depended on size, not typeface ([Hou et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9376262/)). WCAG requires **4.5:1 for normal text** and sets **7:1 as the enhanced level** ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). The site's body color (`--muted`, 78% navy) measures about **7.3:1 on ivory**, so body text already meets the enhanced level. The link color (`--brass`, #c4783a) measures **3.0:1 on ivory and fails**. The darker `--brass-ink` (#91592b) measures 5.0:1 and passes. Low contrast is the most common accessibility failure on the web, found on **83.9% of the top million home pages in 2026** ([WebAIM Million 2026](https://webaim.org/projects/million/)).

**Lines and spacing follow the standards, because no older-adult test exists for them.** WCAG's enhanced level asks for lines no wider than **80 characters**, no justified text, and line spacing of at least 1.5 ([W3C, Understanding 1.4.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/visual-presentation.html)). Baymard recommends **50 to 75 characters**, set with a CSS width of about 70ch. In its tests, users found long lines "intimidating" and tired on lines over 100 characters ([Baymard 2022](https://baymard.com/blog/line-length-readability)). Older adults read better with more line spacing in one study ([Hou et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9376262/)). The site's paragraphs have a 720-pixel maximum width and a line height of 1.75. At 15.5 pixels, 720 pixels holds about 90 characters, using the common estimate that an average character is half the font size wide. Raising the size to 20 pixels on desktop brings the same column to about 72 characters. Setting `max-width: 65ch` fixes it at any size.

**Tap targets must be large and spaced.** WCAG sets a minimum of **24 by 24 CSS pixels** for any target, and **44 by 44** at the enhanced level. W3C names hand tremor among the conditions this helps and says to aim for 44 on important controls ([W3C, Understanding 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)). Links inside a sentence are exempt. NN/g recommends touch targets of at least 1 cm by 1 cm, because average fingertips measure 1.6 to 2 cm wide ([NN/g 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/)). NN/g's senior studies found that tightly grouped links cause wrong clicks ([NN/g 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)).

**Nothing should move, pop up or hide by itself.** WCAG requires a pause control for anything that moves for more than 5 seconds, and forbids a change of context when an item gets focus ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). W3C ties the second rule to pop-ups and new windows for older users ([W3C older users](https://www.w3.org/WAI/older-users/developing/)). Older participants installed ad blockers to stop pop-ups and ads that play sound ([NN/g 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/)). Forms that rejected dashes or parentheses in phone numbers caused failures, and older users blamed themselves for the errors ([NN/g 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/)). W3C also lists reduced short-term memory and easy distraction as age-related changes ([W3C WAI](https://www.w3.org/WAI/older-users/)). A fixed order of sections across pages lowers that load. W3C's consistent-navigation rule and NN/g's advice against drastic redesigns both support it ([W3C older users](https://www.w3.org/WAI/older-users/developing/); [NN/g 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)).

Little of this evidence isolates people aged 55 to 64. Most senior studies use 65 and older. NN/g's paid report with 87 guidelines was not read. No study was found on icons with labels, sticky headers, accordions or tables of contents for older adults specifically. Those rules below rest on all-age tests and standards.

## Readers see a fifth of the page, so the first screen must hold the answer

**Readers scan headings first.** NN/g's eyetracking work describes a "layer-cake" pattern: the eyes jump from heading to heading and read the body only under the heading they want. NN/g calls it "by far the most effective way" to scan, and it depends on headings that say something ([NN/g 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)). Without clear headings, readers fall into the F-pattern and miss content on the right and lower down ([NN/g 2017](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). Headings should be short, descriptive and start with the words that carry the meaning, because they also appear alone in search results ([NN/g 2015](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/headings-pickup-lines/)). A summary of NN/g's work adds that when list items or questions start with the same words, readers skip those words. In FAQ lists they skip "How" and "Why" ([UX Planet summary](https://uxplanet.org/people-dont-read-online-they-scan-this-is-how-to-write-for-them-80a75069c14e)). That source is secondary. Its practical meaning is to put the place or topic early in a question heading.

**The fold still matters.** The 100 pixels just above the fold drew **102% more viewing** than the 100 pixels just below it ([NN/g 2015](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/)). A full-width band that looks like the end of the page stops scrolling; NN/g calls it a "false floor". On a phone, a large hero photo above the answer pushes the answer down and can act as one. Large images on phones are also mistaken for ads and skipped ([NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/)). Hard text is harder on a phone. In one test, readers scored **18.9% on a comprehension test on a phone-sized screen against 39.2% on desktop** ([NN/g 2011](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content-is-twice-as-difficult-2011/)). Tax, insurance and HOA material needs short sentences and tables on a phone.

**A labeled summary and a table of contents help readers decide.** In NN/g's long-form study, a summary at the top worked best for judging whether a page is relevant. Summaries at the end were rarely found ([NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/formatting-long-form-content/)). NN/g's table of contents guide gives specific rules ([NN/g 2023, table of contents](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/table-of-contents/)). Put it near the top of the body. Label it "On this page". Make each link label identical to its section heading, and list every section. Make the links look like links: in testing, users ignored a list with uncolored links. Do not make it sticky, because on phones "many users failed to notice the sticky table of contents". Add "back to top" links on long pages. Bankrate's guide shows why labels must match: its list says "Affordability considerations" while the heading says "Affordability issues to consider" ([Bankrate](https://www.bankrate.com/real-estate/how-to-buy-a-house/)). A search of the website clone found **no article with an "On this page" list**.

**Short blocks and little bold.** GOV.UK asks for sentences under 25 words and paragraphs of five sentences or fewer ([GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)). NN/g says bold should cover no more than 30% of an article, with bold reserved for the key points ([NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/formatting-long-form-content/)). In a study of plain English, 80% of people preferred clear sentences, and the preference grew with the reader's education ([GDS blog, 2014](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2014/02/17/guest-post-clarity-is-king-the-evidence-that-reveals-the-desperate-need-to-re-think-the-way-we-write/)). Plain writing suits retirees and investors who know real estate as much as first-time buyers.

**Keep main content open.** NN/g says content hidden in an accordion "may be missed altogether", and advises against hiding crucial information in collapsed panels. Accordions fit FAQs on small screens, if several can be open at once ([NN/g 2023, accordions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accordions-on-desktop/)). Carousels are mostly scrolled past, and only the first frame is reliably seen ([NN/g 2013, carousels](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)). Sticky headers take screen space, most of all on phones. NN/g advises a small header that hides when the reader scrolls down and returns when they scroll up ([NN/g 2021](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)).

**CTAs work best when they look like the page.** NN/g's 2018 eyetracking found users ignored inline promotions such as "a blue rectangle placed between blocks of text". Colored backgrounds, text inside images and animation all trigger this ([NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/)). Attention is highest near the top ([NN/g 2018, scrolling](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). Stanford's credibility guidelines ask for restraint with promotion ([Stanford, Fogg 2002](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/index.html)). No study measures the best number or position of CTAs inside an article. Claims such as "a CTA at the bottom converts 220% better" are single vendor tests ([WPMU DEV](https://wpmudev.com/blog/?p=163473)). The evidence supports a few CTAs, each tied to the section around it, set as a sentence and a plain button, and never as a colored banner or pop-up.

**Trust comes from real people, dates and sources.** In a study of 2,684 people judging live sites, "design look" was the most mentioned factor, in 46.1% of comments ([Consumer WebWatch 2002](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility/)). Stanford's guidelines ask sites to make information easy to verify, show the real organization and people, make contact easy, show the content was recently reviewed and avoid errors ([Stanford](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/index.html)). Users spent about 10% more time on real staff photos than on biographies that took 316% more space ([NN/g 2010](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)). Third-party reviews are trusted more than testimonials on a company's own site ([NN/g 2016](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)).

**What leading sites do.** NerdWallet and Bankrate both show a named writer and editor with job titles near the top, a date, and a disclosure of how they make money ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/mortgages/learn/how-to-keep-from-being-house-poor); [Bankrate](https://www.bankrate.com/real-estate/how-to-buy-a-house/)). Bankrate adds a three-point "Key takeaways" box, a short table of contents and FAQs. NerdWallet uses question headings and a "Fact Checked" note. Neither page has a sources list; both use inline links only. Redfin's guide has a comparison table and FAQs, but it places lender promotion boxes and listing cards inside the article ([Redfin](https://www.redfin.com/blog/how-to-buy-a-house/)). On community pages, 55places puts a labeled fact block near the top: price range, HOA dues, number of homes, age rule, gated, pets and builder. It also prints raw database labels such as "OwnerAllowedGolfCart", says "Gated: No" in one place and lists "Gated" in another, repeats headings, opens with a "1 of 19" photo gallery, and shows a 5.0 rating from one review ([55places](https://www.55places.com/south-carolina/communities/del-webb-at-grande-dunes-myrtle-beach)). Del Webb's Myrtle Beach page gives a phone number with its hours next to it ([Del Webb](https://www.delwebb.com/homes/south-carolina/myrtle-beach)). Zillow's redesign moved to short summaries with links to detail pages. Its designer reports that **full-page scroll-through rose from 12% to 33%**, with no method published ([Knecht](https://www.mpknecht.com/zillow-home-detail-page-redesign)). Chapter3 can copy the fact block, the byline pattern and the phone-with-hours pattern. It can do better with one checked source per fact, no contradictions, a visible sources list and no promotion inside the reading line.

## Google and answer engines reward the same well-built page

**Google's 2026 position is plain.** "There are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary" ([Google AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)). Google's May 2026 guide says AI search is "rooted in our core Search ranking and quality systems". It says there is no need to break content into small pieces and "no ideal page length". It says llms.txt "will neither harm nor help" in Google Search ([Google AI guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). The guide uses a real estate example. It calls "7 Tips for First-Time Homebuyers" commodity content "which could originate from anyone". It calls "Why We Waived the Inspection & Saved Money: A Look Inside the Sewer Line" non-commodity content with "unique expert or experienced takes". It says writing many pages for small query variations can break the scaled content abuse policy. For this site, that means local facts and real experience, on one page per question.

**Core Web Vitals are a modest ranking input.** The targets are **LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP under 200 milliseconds and CLS below 0.1** ([Google Core Web Vitals](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals)). They are measured at the 75th percentile of real visits, separately for phones and desktops ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)). Google says relevance comes first, and page experience helps when many pages are equally useful. It also says a perfect score "just for SEO reasons may not be the best use of your time" ([Google page experience](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/page-experience)). Google indexes the phone version of a page. Primary content must not load only after a tap or swipe ([Google mobile-first](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/mobile/mobile-sites-mobile-first-indexing)). Full-screen pop-ups "may lead to poor search performance" ([Google interstitials](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/avoid-intrusive-interstitials)).

**Visible text helps Google's section links.** Google's "Read more" links jump to a section from a search result. They need the content to be "immediately visible on the page to a human (and not hidden behind an expandable section or tabbed interface)", no script that forces the scroll on load, and an intact URL hash ([Google snippets](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/snippet)). Google ranks individual passages of a page ([Google ranking systems](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ranking-systems-guide)). Google says heading order does not matter for ranking, but it matters for screen readers ([Google SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide)). In October 2026 Google's helpful-content page added that "main content" includes tabbed or expanded sections, calculators, titles and headings ([Google helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)). So hidden text still counts as content, but it loses the section links and may be skipped by other engines.

**Microsoft gives the most concrete guidance on quotable structure.** Copilot breaks pages into pieces and judges each one. Microsoft asks for headings that are questions, answers of one or two sentences, "sentences that make sense even when pulled out of context", lists, comparison tables and Q&A pairs, which "assistants can often lift ... word for word". It warns against walls of text, key facts only in images or PDFs, and important answers "in tabs or expandable menus", because "AI systems may not render hidden content". It also says to use em dashes with care ([Microsoft, 2025](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/october-2025/optimizing-your-content-for-inclusion-in-ai-search-answers)). This does not conflict with Google. Google warns against splitting pages. Microsoft asks for sections that stand alone inside one page.

**OpenAI and Perplexity publish crawler rules only.** ChatGPT search needs OAI-SearchBot allowed; a site that blocks it is not shown in ChatGPT search answers ([OpenAI crawlers](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots)). Perplexity needs PerplexityBot ([Perplexity crawlers](https://docs.perplexity.ai/guides/bots)). Neither publishes page design guidance.

**Sourced numbers are the one tested factor.** In the GEO study, adding citations, quotations and statistics raised a page's visibility in generated answers by over 40%. Keyword stuffing did not help ([Aggarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735)). The site's rule against invented quotes still holds; quotations must be real. Industry data shows that **76% of pages cited in Google AI Overviews also rank in Google's top 10** ([Ahrefs 2025](https://ahrefs.com/blog/search-rankings-ai-citations/)). A later Ahrefs study reportedly found 38%, but it was seen only through press coverage and its method differs ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-ai-overview-citations-from-top-ranking-pages-drop-sharply/568637/)). The conflict is unresolved. Ordinary ranking remains the main route into AI Overviews. A 2025 paper found AI engines favor third-party "earned media" over a brand's own pages ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.08919)). A small brokerage does best on local, first-hand questions where few third-party pages exist. Pages cited by AI assistants were 25.7% fresher than organic results, and ChatGPT showed the strongest preference for recent pages ([Ahrefs freshness study](https://ahrefs.com/blog/do-ai-assistants-prefer-to-cite-fresh-content/)). Google lists changing dates on unchanged pages as a warning sign ([Google helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)). The date changes only when the prose changes.

## Maps and photos are worth their cost only when they show a fact

**The first large image is usually the slowest part of the page.** It is often the Largest Contentful Paint element. web.dev says it must be in the HTML, must not be lazy-loaded, and should carry `fetchpriority="high"` ([web.dev LCP](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-lcp); [web.dev fetch priority](https://web.dev/articles/fetch-priority)). Pages that lazy-loaded too much had a slower LCP in field data, 3,546 against 2,922 milliseconds at the 75th percentile ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/lcp-lazy-loading)). Images below the first screen should use `loading="lazy"` with a width and height ([web.dev lazy loading](https://web.dev/articles/browser-level-image-lazy-loading)). Serve three to five sizes with `srcset` ([web.dev responsive images](https://web.dev/articles/serve-responsive-images)) and use AVIF or WebP with a JPEG fallback ([web.dev formats](https://web.dev/articles/choose-the-right-image-format)). Google indexes `<img>` elements, not CSS backgrounds, and alt text is "the most important attribute" ([Google image SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/google-images)). Google recommends Article images of at least 50,000 pixels in 16:9, 4:3 and 1:1 shapes ([Google Article](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/article)). The repo's 1200-pixel minimum meets that.

**A live map costs far more than a picture of a map.** Popular embeds carry over 100 KB of script, sometimes up to 2 MB ([web.dev embeds](https://web.dev/articles/embed-best-practices)). web.dev recommends replacing a Google Maps iframe with a static image linked to the full map, or loading it only on a click, because "not every user ... will click, expand, scroll, and navigate the map embed". No measured cost of a Google Maps iframe on phones was found. A Lighthouse test of one page with and without it would close that gap. The repo's image rules ban Google Maps images and screenshots (`rules/images.md`), so the static picture must be the site's own map from `tools/area-map.js`. Microsoft warns against facts that appear only in images ([Microsoft](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/october-2025/optimizing-your-content-for-inclusion-in-ai-search-answers)). The drive times on the map must also appear in the text.

**Captions are read, and decorative photos are skipped.** In news eyetracking, readers looked at text first, and captions were well read ([Editor and Publisher summary](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/online-news-readers-prefer-text-over-graphics,19526)). Longer captions drew more attention to the photo ([CJF summary](https://cjf-fjc.ca/globe-public-editor-just-few-words-photo-cutlines-have-enormous-impact/)). Both are trade-press summaries of older news studies. A caption should state the fact the image shows. Readers ignored decorative and stock images ([NN/g 2010](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)). The common claim that white space raises comprehension by 20% has no primary source and should not be repeated.

**Icons need words.** No study was found that tests icons with and without labels in article content. The safe rule is an icon beside a visible word, never an icon alone. WCAG requires meaningful graphics to reach 3:1 contrast against their background ([WCAG 2.2, 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). Key facts sit in HTML text next to the icon, not inside an image.

**Fonts can shift the page.** `font-display: optional` causes no layout shift from font swapping. `swap` shows text at once but shifts the page if the font arrives late ([web.dev fonts](https://web.dev/articles/font-best-practices)). The website uses `swap` on 21 font faces. The repo's STANDARD H11 records a measured font-swap shift of 0.33 to 0.38 on two pages, more than three times Google's 0.1 limit.

**Video does not earn video results on a guide page.** Google gives video features only to a page whose main purpose is one video ([Google video SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/video)). A guide page embeds a video only for readers, behind a click-to-load facade.

## The playbook: order, numbers and changes to the repo

### Community guide page, top to bottom

This order is for a 55+ community, a neighborhood or a town. It follows CRAFT section 3: the good news first, then what the reader needs to picture it, then cost and risk, then the next step. Two places can still differ inside the sections.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Large photo above the short answer | Pushes the answer below the fold and reads as an ad on phones ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/)). | Key facts row first; photos lower, with captions. |
| Body text under 16 px, or gray body text | Named barrier for older users ([NN/g 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/)). | 18 px, near-black. |
| Links without underline, or in light brass | Seniors miss links; brass fails 4.5:1. | Underline, `--brass-ink`. |
| FAQ or key answers in closed accordions or tabs | Missed by readers, may be skipped by Copilot, no Google section links ([Microsoft](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/october-2025/optimizing-your-content-for-inclusion-in-ai-search-answers)). | Open text. |
| Photo carousel or gallery that rotates | Only the first frame is seen ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)). | Two or three static captioned photos. |
| Pop-up lead form on arrival | Harms search and annoys older readers ([Google](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/avoid-intrusive-interstitials)). | Inline form at the end. |
| CTA in a colored box repeated down the page | Banner blindness; breaks the restraint guideline. | A sentence and a plain button tied to the section. |
| Table of contents labels that differ from headings | Readers lose their place (Bankrate example). | Copy the H2 text exactly. |
| Facts only in a map, chart or photo | Engines and screen readers cannot read them ([Microsoft](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/october-2025/optimizing-your-content-for-inclusion-in-ai-search-answers)). | Repeat the fact in the text. |
| Live map iframe loading on every visit | Adds script and slows phones ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/embed-best-practices)). | Static own map with a link. |
| Lazy-loading the first image | Delays LCP ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/lcp-lazy-loading)). | Lazy-load only below the first screen. |
| Raw labels, contradictions, repeated headings (55places) | Lowers trust and scanning. | One fact, one wording, one heading per topic. |
| Star rating from one review | Signals little; self-made ratings are not eligible for rich results ([Google](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet)). | Show no rating. |
| Changing the date without changing the page | Google lists it as a warning sign. | Date only from `build.js dates` after a prose change. |
| Generic tips page with no local facts | Google's own example of commodity content. | Local facts and first-hand experience. |
| Phone field that rejects dashes or spaces | Older users fail and blame themselves. | Accept any format. |

### Checklist for a built page

Test on a phone at 375 px wide, a tablet, and a desktop. Test once with the phone's largest text setting.

| # | Check | Passes when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First screen on a phone | H1, byline and the first sentence of the short answer show without scrolling. |

| 5 | Contrast | Body 7:1 or better; every other text 4.5:1; icons and borders 3:1 (axe at 375 and 1280). |
| 6 | Links | Underlined, 4.5:1, link text names the destination. |

| 11 | Short answer | 40 to 120 words, verdict first, reads correctly when quoted alone. |
| 12 | Key facts row | Community pages: 5 to 8 labeled facts, each icon with a word. |

| 20 | Map | Own static map with caption and live-map link; drive times also in the text. |
| 21 | Table | At least one real HTML table where options compare; readable at 375 px. |
| 22 | Bold | No section more than 30% bold. |
| 23 | Story | One, 5 to 8 sentences, real or labelled "Example", after the facts. |
| 24 | CTAs | Each worded for its section; no colored banner; no repeat; phone button with hours. |
| 25 | Form | Accepts any phone format; clear error next to the field; sends (STANDARD H12). |
| 26 | FAQ | 4 to 8 open entries, answer first, distinct question openers. |
| 27 | Trust | Byline, reviewer, build date, author note, inline source links, sources line, disclosure, contact details. |
| 28 | Structured data | Article with Person author and both dates; BreadcrumbList; every marked fact visible; FAQPage matches word for word if present. |
| 29 | Speed | Phone field data (or lab proxy before launch): LCP 2.5 s or less, INP under 200 ms, CLS under 0.1. |
| 30 | Crawlers | Googlebot, Bingbot, OAI-SearchBot and PerplexityBot allowed. |

Several of these checks can be added to `tools/score.js` with a passing and a failing control: table of contents labels against H2 text, bold share per section, first-image lazy loading, FAQ inside `<details>`, and links without underlines.

## Conclusion

The research points to one page that serves readers aged 55 to 75, Google and the answer engines together. No separate "AEO layout" is needed. Google said so directly in 2026, and Microsoft's requests (question headings, short self-contained answers, open text, tables) describe a page that a careful older reader also finds easiest. The repo's writing rules are already ahead of most published guidance. The main gap is visual. The site's article text is smaller and its lines are longer than the evidence supports. Its links fail contrast, and it has no table of contents. Its FAQ answers are hidden on hub pages, and its map rule still allows the slowest kind of map. These are CSS and template changes, not rewrites, so they can reach every page at once.

The weakest parts of the evidence should guide what gets measured next. No study isolates readers aged 55 to 64. None tests icons, tables of contents or CTA counts with older adults. None measures what a Google Maps embed costs on a phone. The cheapest way to close these gaps is the site's own data: a Lighthouse run with and without a map, and a short session with a few local retirees reading one page on their own phones. Those results should outrank any general rule in this report.
