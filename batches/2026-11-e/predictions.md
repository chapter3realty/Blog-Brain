# What past examples show: batch 2026-11-e

Each section names a policy one of the winners proposed, the past examples we could verify, what happened in the short run (first 1-2 years) and the long run (5+ years), and the honest range. Every statement is tied to ledger rows in `facts.md`. Where no past example was verified, we say so and make no estimate. None of this is a forecast of what the new officials will pass. Each plan is a proposal [F031, F060].

## 1. A federal first-time buyer tax credit (Andrews backs the $50,000 idea) [page 2]

**What is proposed.** H.R. 3475: a refundable credit equal to the down payment, up to $50,000, with income limits and a five-year clawback, plus a builder credit for small starter homes [F102, F103]. It has not had a committee vote [F104]. Andrews backs it but has no bill of her own [F031].

**Past example A: the 2008-2010 federal credit** [F106-F110].
- Short run, nationally: sales rose by about 490,000 (9.8%); median prices rose about $2,400 (1.1%) per standard deviation of a ZIP code's exposure to the program; the move into ownership rose 53% [F110].
- Timing: demand was pulled forward "from several years in the future"; buyers were about three years younger than usual [F110]. The authors judge the stabilizing benefit larger than the direct stimulus [F110].
- Short run, locally: the Myrtle Beach metro price index fell 9.7% from 2008 Q4 to 2009 Q4 and kept falling to 2012 Q4 [F199]. The credit did not stop a local decline. We cannot say how much deeper the fall would have been without it; the study is national.
- Long run: no verified source on prices 5+ years later tied to the credit. Locally the index recovered only after 2012 [F199, F198]; we cannot attribute that to the credit.
- Size difference: the old credit topped out at $8,000 [F106]; the proposal is up to $50,000 [F102], about six times larger. The study's effects were measured at the smaller size. Do not scale them up; no verified source does.

**Past example B: the D.C. credit, $5,000, to 2011** [F111]. No verified study of its price effect found. Use only as proof that a smaller targeted credit existed.

**Past example C: Biden's 2024 $10,000 proposal** [F112]. Treasury scored the buyer credit at $31.0 billion over 10 years. It never became law [F112]. This shows how a credit of this kind is costed, not what it did.

**Honest range.** If a credit like H.R. 3475 passed, the only verified evidence (a credit one-sixth the size) points to more sales and slightly higher prices in the first years, with some buyers simply moving their purchase earlier [F110]. In a market where prices were already falling, the local index kept falling anyway [F199]. Whether a much larger credit would raise prices by more than it helps buyers is not answered by any source we verified. Do not say it will.

## 4. NFIP reauthorization and lapses [page 3]

**What is pending.** The NFIP expires Dec. 11, 2026 [F120], before Andrews takes office [F001].
**Past examples: 36 short extensions since 2017 and the lapses in 2010, 2018, 2024, 2025 and 2026** [F122, F123, F124].
- Short run: during a lapse, no new NFIP policies can be written [F121]. In June 2010, over 1,400 closings a day were canceled or delayed nationwide [F124]. During the Oct.-Nov. 2025 lapse, some lenders waived the flood insurance requirement [F124]. Every lapse so far has ended with Congress restoring the program, the longest about six weeks (Oct. 1 to Nov. 12, 2025) [F123].
- Long run: the program has run on short extensions for nine years [F122]; we found no verified source on long-run price effects of that.
- Premium path: by law most premiums rise at most 18% a year [F128]; subsidized second-home rates rise 25% a year until they reach full risk [F129]. These limits apply whoever is in the Senate.
**Honest range.** For a buyer in a flood zone who needs a loan, a lapse can delay a closing until Congress acts or the lender accepts private coverage [F124, F125]. The past range is a few hours to six weeks [F123]. One new senator does not change the Dec. 11 date [F120, F001].

## 5. Wilson: remove the higher tax on rentals (Act 388 reform) [pages 4 and 5]

**What is proposed.** A campaign line with no bill, ratio or cost [F060]. The 6% ratio is in the constitution; changing it needs two-thirds of each house [F080, F081], and the House count after this election is short of two-thirds for Republicans alone [F083].
**Worked effect on one house (arithmetic, not a forecast).** A $350,000 Myrtle Beach rental pays $5,346.60 at 6%; $3,564.40 at 4% if it still pays school operating tax; $2,037.00 if it were taxed like a residence [F145-F147].
**Past example A: Act 388 itself (2006)**, which cut owner-occupied school taxes and raised the sales tax [F140, F141, F086].
- Short and long run, the strongest source on the burden: the Lincoln Institute study (project partners the SC Chamber Foundation and SC Realtors) found the burden shifted toward non-owner-occupied homes and business property [F150, F151]. In Horry in 2018, non-owner-occupied homes were 36.4% of appraised value but 47.2% of assessed value [F149].
- The other side: Act 388's defenders (for example nohometax.org, cited in the USC note) support the homeowner exemption [F152]. We did not open their page; the writer should not state their arguments beyond the name until it is verified.
- Rents: the USC law note says the rental rate has led to "rising rent costs" [F152]. That note is a student note citing other sources, not a measured study. Present it as a claim, with who made it.
**Past example B: Florida's 10% cap on non-homestead assessments** [F158]. It limits yearly increases rather than cutting the ratio. We found no verified study of its effect on Florida rents or prices. Do not claim one.
**Honest range.** If the ratio for rentals fell from 6% to 4%, the arithmetic says a Horry rental's bill would fall by about a third (city example) [F146]. Who makes up the lost revenue (owners, businesses, the state) is not in any plan we read [F060]. No verified source says whether the savings would reach tenants or show up in prices. Short-run legal path: two-thirds votes or a 2028 amendment vote [F081, F084].

## 6. State preemption of local short-term rental rules [page 6]

**What could happen.** A bill like H. 3861 would stop local bans and penalize governments that keep them [F170, F171]. It died in committee [F170]. No winning candidate took a position [F180].
**Past example A: Arizona.** 2016 law barred local bans [F175]. In 2022 the legislature let cities require permits, contacts and insurance while keeping the ban on bans [F176]. Short run: local governments lost the power to ban; six years later the state gave some tools back [F175, F176]. We found no verified study of Arizona prices or rents from the change.
**Past example B: Florida.** 2011 law barred new local bans and limits on rental duration, but kept rules adopted before June 1, 2011 [F177]. In 2024 the governor vetoed a bill to move more control to the state [F178]. No verified price study.
**Past example C: local limits, Los Angeles County** (the reverse direction) [F179]. Cities that restricted STRs cut listings by 50% and house prices and rents by about 2% [F179].
**Honest range.** Myrtle Beach already bars STRs in most residential zones [F172]. If a state law forced those zones open, the LA evidence (read in reverse) suggests prices and rents in affected neighborhoods could rise a few percent; that is an inference from a different market and policy direction, so say it that way or leave it out [F179]. If nothing passes, local rules stay as they are [F170].

## 7. Roads, I-73 and permits [page 7]

**What is known.** RIDE IV passed in 2024 [F190]; its I-73 money depends on SCDOT signing contracts [F191]; I-73 has no construction funding [F192]. Permits for single-family homes in Horry stayed between 6,356 and 7,269 a year from 2021 to 2025 [F196].
**Past example: Horry County's own permit history** [F196, F197]. After the 2007-2008 slowdown, single-family permits fell from 6,484 (2006) to 1,388 (2010) and took until 2021 to return above 6,000 [F196]. This shows permits follow the housing cycle; it does not show any effect of a road, a fee or an election.
**Impact fees.** The 2026 schedule sets about $1,615 for a 1,500-square-foot home [F194]. No verified local study of the fee's effect on prices or permits. The builders' association says fees can mean fewer homes [F194]; that is an interest group's view.
**Honest range.** No verified past example ties a governor's or senator's road plan to Horry permit counts. Make no estimate.

## 8. Elections and Myrtle Beach prices in general [page 1]

**Past examples: the four elections the brief named** [F198].
- Year after 2016: +5.6%. After 2020: +21.6%. After 2022: +8.5%. After 2024: +3.0%. (Plus 2018: +5.0%.)
- The changes differ by a factor of seven from one election to the next. We have no verified row that explains why; do not attribute them to the winners.
**Honest range.** The local record shows the year after an election has ranged from +3.0% to +21.6% [F198], and the latest year is +0.8% [F200]. Nothing in it lets anyone say this election will move prices up or down.
