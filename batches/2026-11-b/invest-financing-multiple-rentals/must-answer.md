## Who searched this, and why

An owner of one rental, or of a home they will keep and rent out, who wants the next one. Often they live in another state. They know how their first loan went. They do not know four things:
- where the down payment comes from if not from savings;
- whether a lender here will lend against the rental they own;
- how much cash the lender wants left in the bank afterwards;
- whether there is a limit on how many loans they can have.

They worry that a second house turns one good rental into two bad ones. The next thing they ask is "would my numbers work?" That is the offer.

What this reader would pay an expert to tell them:
- How much cash the first house can give them, in dollars, for a house bought when theirs was.
- Which way of taking equity out lenders here actually make: a cash-out refinance on the rental, or a line of credit on the home they live in.
- Why lenders here turn down loans on rentals, so they can avoid it.
- What cash they must keep after closing, as a number.
- What the two houses cost them if both sit empty.

## The deciding fact

**The standard rules let borrowed money secured by a home you own pay the down payment, the closing costs and the reserves on the next one** (Fannie Mae B3-4.3-15, re-opened today; facts row 3). On a one-unit rental, a cash-out refinance can take the new loan to three quarters of the appraised value (Freddie Mac's table, row 1; dollars only on the page). Both hold today. Without them the page's answer ("the equity in what you own") could not be written.

The local fact that decides how the page is built: **in 2025, lenders reported 1,228 lines of credit on Horry County homes their owners live in and 17 on rentals** (HMDA, row 34). On a rental, the cash-out refinance is the route lenders here make (149 in 2025). A line of credit on the home you live in is the other route the rules accept (row 3). The page leads with these two routes.

## The must-answer list, in order

1. **How do you finance a second rental here? Two sentences.** From savings, or from the equity in a home you own: a cash-out refinance on the rental, or a line of credit on the home you live in. The lender then wants the new rent to cover the new house and cash left over. (A, B; rows 1, 3, 34.)
2. **How much cash can my first rental give me?** In dollars, by the year it was bought: a typical house bought in 2019 has gained about $130,400; one bought in 2022 to 2024 about nothing. The largest new loan on the example, in dollars. The 12-month and six-month timing. (B; rows 1, 2, 16, 17.)
3. **Can I get a line of credit (HELOC) on a rental?** Hard here: 17 made on rentals in 2025, and about 6 in 10 decided applications turned down; on homes owners live in, 1,228 made. A line on the home you live in can pay the next down payment under the standard rules. Its payment then counts as a debt. (C, D; rows 3, 34, 35, 36.)
4. **Is it hard to get a loan on a rental here, and why do lenders say no?** About 1 in 7 rental purchase applications that reached a decision was turned down in 2025. The reason named most was the home itself (its value or type), then debt-to-income, then not enough cash. (D; rows 37, 38.)
5. **How much cash must I keep (reserves) when I own other financed homes?** Six months of the new house's full monthly cost, plus 2, 4 or 6 percent of what you owe on your other financed homes. On the example, about $8,400 plus six months of the new loan's payment and insurance. The lender only checks it is there. (E; rows 11, 12, 13, 20.)
6. **What does the lender count of the new rent?** Three quarters of a long-term rent; half of a nightly rental's market rent, only against its own cost; a year as a landlord changes what the rent can do. (G; rows 5, 6, 7, 18.)
7. **The worst case as a number, and the three mistakes.** Two houses empty three months: about $2,950 in tax and dues, about $13,400 of rent at HUD's figure, plus the loan payments and insurance. The story, told once. (Owner's brief; rows 21, 22, 28 to 30.)
8. **How many rentals can you finance?** Ten on the standard path, counting your own home if it has a mortgage; a loan you are not personally on does not count. One short section halfway down, as the owner asked. (F; rows 9, 10.)
9. **What the second house's tax is here.** 6 percent, about 3.3 times an owner-occupant's bill outside city limits; about $4,700 a year on the example. (Row 23.)
10. **Are other people here doing this?** About 1 in 20 home sales in the last 12 months went to a buyer who bought two or more; about 6,000 owners hold two or more homes taxed at 6 percent. (Rows 24 to 26.)
11. **What we do next.** We run the next house with both loans on it before you offer, and put you with a loan officer at our preferred lender. (Row 32.)
