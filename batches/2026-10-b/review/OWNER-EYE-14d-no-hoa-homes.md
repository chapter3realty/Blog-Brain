## High findings

**High 1. The entries do not "speak extensively on all of the benefits of that location and why people like it."**
This is the owner's main sentence for this page, and the entries got shorter: median 103 words, down from 120. Three entries sell nothing:
- **2. Myrtle Heights** ($1.6 million, the most expensive area on the page). Here is the whole sale: "The Myrtle Heights-Oak Park Historic District is along Ocean Boulevard ... The district's houses were built from about 1925 to 1945. Colonial Revival is the most common style ... Two of the houses for sale were built before 1950, and a new oceanfront house was listed too." Then "The trade-off is price." Nothing says why someone pays $1.6 million to live there.
- **4. Plantation Point** (74 words, the shortest). "Plantation Point is outside the Myrtle Beach city limits, on streets such as Annandale, Arundel and Brookgreen Drive. Its lots are typically about half an acre ... Plantation Point has a voluntary HOA ... The beach is not close." The only thing it says about the place is bad news. This is the same field list the round 14 review flagged.
- **1. Downtown Myrtle Beach.** A boardwalk, a 1937 train depot, house years by street, a listing note ("eight new houses ... had no HOA fee in their listings either"), and a lot size. Nothing says what living a few blocks from the ocean in town gives you.

Most of the rest give history and a distance, then stop. Pine Lakes (88 words) explains the big lots well, then says nothing about life there. Conway (80 words) has the live oaks and the Riverwalk, then two lines. Surfside gives the boat rule, a walk, a playground 0.1 mile away, trash pickup and flood. No entry says what an owner does on a normal Saturday, and few say why people choose the place over the next one.

Do instead: bring every entry to at least the depth of Cherry Grove and Little River (about 130 to 160 words). Keep the order the round 14 brief set: the best thing a buyer could not guess, then a normal day and why people choose it, then one trade-off. Kinds of fact to research, per place:
- where owners eat, fish, launch or walk on a weekend;
- what the older houses are like (porches, carports, garages, yards big enough for a shop);
- what the place is known for locally, beyond its history;
- for Plantation Point, why owners chose half-acre lots there over the beach streets;
- for Myrtle Heights, what a house in a historic beach district gives you that a newer oceanfront house does not.

Each new fact needs a row in the ledger.

**High 2. 3.2 phone screens before the first area.**
Better than 4.1, but over the "about two" the brief and the owner-eye prompt set. The map card alone is 1.3 screens on a phone (three panels stacked: "The whole area", "A. Myrtle Beach to Garden City", "B. North Myrtle Beach").
Do instead:
- On a phone, show one map: the two coast close-ups with every area named. Drop "The whole area" panel to a small inset, or below the guide. Conway, Loris, Aynor and the country around Loris can be one line of names under the map.
- Cut the dek. "Older beach streets, river towns and country land from Little River to Aynor, where you can keep a boat or a work truck at home" says again what the H1's second line and the guide say.
- Target: entry 1 within about 2 screens of the H1, as on affordable (2.3) and gated (2.5).

**High 3. The new rules table is the most useful section, and a buyer cannot read it.**
The table answers what this reader came for: a boat, a truck, renting, a shed. But many cells are zone codes or non-answers:
- "Only in the CC, CBD and WRD zones" (Conway);
- "Only in the R-3 and C-3 zones" (Surfside);
- "Listed for the RC and GRR zones" (Georgetown County);
- "Not named in county zoning" (Horry County) and "Not named in town zoning" (Aynor). Can I rent or not?
- "Not limited by zoning in general" (North Myrtle Beach);
- "Untagged trailers only in a building" (Georgetown County). Can a tagged boat sit in the yard?

On a phone the table becomes 8 cards with 32 "Label: value" lines, 2.5 screens long. This is the owner's "entries that nobody will ever understand", in a new place.
Do instead:
- Answer each cell yes or no, then the limit, in plain words. For example: "Yes, in the downtown and riverfront business areas; not on most home streets." Never print a zone code. The code goes in the ledger.
- Where a code is silent, say what that means for the buyer, as a verified row allows: "The county code does not address it; we check the lot's zoning before you buy." If a verifier will not allow a meaning, say "We check this for the lot you choose."
- Cut the Georgetown County row unless an entry is in Georgetown County. No entry on the page is placed there, so the row reads as noise.
- On a phone, let each town's card say four short sentences, not four label lines.

**High 4. Twelve of 16 areas have no picture.**
Entries 1 to 7 and 9 to 12, and 15, are text only. That is seven entries in a row (1 to 7), 7.7 phone screens of plain text, before the first photo at Cherry Grove. At 1280 px each entry is a narrow column with the right half of the screen empty. The writer cut the three unclear aerials (Pine Lakes, Little River, Surfside), which was right, but put nothing in their place. The page went from 7 pictures to 4.
The shot list for all twelve is already in `batches/2026-10-b/photo-request-abdulla.html`.
Do instead: do not send this page to the owner until the partner's photos are in, at least for the first seven entries. Each photo shows the entry's point, such as an older Myrtle Heights house from the street, or the Ingram Dunes trail. If one area cannot be shot, use a labelled aerial only where the label shows the point at phone width, as the Aynor photo does.

## Medium findings

**Medium 1. Sentences that describe the data, not the place.**
These describe one day's listings or how we measured. The owner: "thats not how a human talks."
- "On 12th Avenue South, eight new houses in The Villages at Withers Heights had no HOA fee in their listings either." (1)
- "Two of the houses for sale were built before 1950, and a new oceanfront house was listed too. From each one, a public beach access is less than 5 minutes away on foot." (2)
- "Each house we placed on the county map is less than half a mile on foot from a public beach access." (6)
- "In Ocean Drive Estates, five houses from the mid-1980s were listed for less than $370,000." (7) It also fights the $665,000 chip above it.
- "Of the 15 houses we placed on the county map, 9 are within a 10-minute walk of a beach access." (7)
- "McLeod Loris hospital is a 9-minute drive from the middle of these houses." (15)
- "The typical house for sale within a mile and a half of downtown was built about 1970." (13)

Do instead: say what the place has. "Most houses here are a short walk from the sand." "Some of the original 1930s houses still stand beside new oceanfront ones." "Older houses here are mostly from the 1960s and 1970s." The method goes in the ledger and the sources line.

**Medium 2. The map names 7 of its 16 areas.**
Conway, Loris, Myrtle Heights, Windy Hill, Crescent Beach, Ocean Drive, Cherry Grove, Surfside and Garden City are bare numbers. Conway and Loris are the two towns a reader is most likely to know.
Do instead: give every area a name pill. On the North Myrtle Beach panel, where 5 to 8 sit close together, put the pills out over the ocean with a short leader line.

**Medium 3. Two kinds of price on one page.**
Fourteen chips say "Houses list for about ..." (asking prices). Cherry Grove and Surfside say "Houses usually sell for about ..." (recorded sales), to match the best neighborhoods page. The sources line says "list prices". A reader comparing Cherry Grove's $695,000 with Ocean Drive's $665,000 is comparing two different measures.
Do instead: use one measure in every chip. If the page must agree with best neighborhoods on Cherry Grove and Surfside, use recorded sales for all 16, or say "asking price" in every chip and accept that the two pages differ. Correct the sources line to match.

**Medium 4. The page tells the reader to check.**
"The zoning office can tell you how a rule applies to a given lot." This is the owner's "we do the work" rule (CRAFT 5).
Do instead: "We check the zoning for the lot before you make an offer."

**Medium 5. More areas, as the owner asked.**
"do that for as many areas as possible." The page is still at 16. The round 14 review asked for the older streets of Murrells Inlet, and they are not here; the FAQ handles Longs and the country around Conway honestly.
Do instead: test more candidates with the same MLS and county data, and add each one where most homes show no fee. Candidates: the older streets of Murrells Inlet, older sections of Forestbrook, Bucksport, Green Sea and Galivants Ferry, and small beach towns along the Grand Strand. Describe each place by its features only (fair housing).

## The best three things: keep them

1. **The two chips under every heading.** "Listed with no HOA fee: nearly 9 in 10 homes for sale" and "Houses list for about $435,000". They give the owner his "all or most have no HOA" and his "one line about the average price" in one glance, in the same place every time. They also leave the first sentence free to sell.
2. **The entries that sell with facts nobody could guess.** Keep them as the model for the rest:
   - Cherry Grove: the city dredges the canals so owners reach "House Creek and the Intracoastal Waterway at high and low tide".
   - Little River: "the first organized offshore trips of that kind in South Carolina", and boats crossing the bar "at any time of day".
   - Loris: the Bog-Off and what chicken bog is.
   - Aynor: "State law names the third Saturday in September for it."
   - The country around Loris: 28 miles of the Waccamaw protected, "with riverside camping", and "room for a boat, a camper and a shop".
   - Pine Lakes: "In 1946, the club sold 18 of its 27 holes for homes and kept nine. That is why the lots are big."
3. **The short answer and the honest FAQ.** "Almost every house for sale outside a named subdivision is listed with no homeowners association (HOA) fee" and the age rule change how a buyer searches. "Longs and the country around Conway look rural, but most homes for sale there have an HOA fee too" saves them a wasted weekend. Keep the Teresa example too; it is clear on one reading.

## Five answers in one line each

1. **Does every entry sell the place like the best agent in town, leading with something a buyer could not guess?** No. Downtown Myrtle Beach (1), Myrtle Heights (2) and Plantation Point (4) do not sell at all. Pine Lakes (3), Surfside Beach (11) and Conway (13) lead well and then stop before they say why people live there.
2. **Is there any "facts:" style label line, or field list in prose?** No "facts:" lines. Plantation Point is a field list in prose, and the rules table on a phone is 32 "Label: value" lines with zone codes.
3. **Does every place end with its own offer?** Yes, 16 of 16: "Get the homes for sale in [area]", linked to the form with the place named.
4. **Does it look modern (liquid glass), with charts that are simple to understand?** The glass chips, map card, rule cards and pill offers are modern. There is no chart. The rules table is not simple to understand, and 12 text-only entries look plain, most of all at 1280 px.
5. **Is any picture unclear?** No. All four show their point. The Loris photo is weak (half sky and traffic lights), not unclear.
