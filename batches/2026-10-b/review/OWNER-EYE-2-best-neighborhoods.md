## Changes I would ask for, most important first

1. **Medium. The overview map has no "Myrtle Beach" on it.** The page is "What are the best neighborhoods in Myrtle Beach?" The map labels Conway, but not the city in the title. The caption says "The city of Myrtle Beach is on the coast around numbers 6, 10 and 12", which makes the reader do the work. The owner: "people out of state don't know roads or anything about the city at all including where this community is."
   - Wanted: a "MYRTLE BEACH" place label on the coast near 6, 10 and 12, like the "CONWAY" label.
   - While there: dots 5, 7, 8, 9, 11 and 15 are numbers only. The key under the map is correct, so this meets the round 3 rule. But 7 is The Farm, the page's own "lower price" pick, and a reader on a phone cannot find it without scrolling. Name it on the map. Name the others where they fit (Waterbridge, Plantation Lakes, Berkshire, Forestbrook, Prince Creek).

2. **Medium. The story has no act by the agent on its problem.**
   - "Example: Megan wants a house near the beach that costs less. A Chapter3 agent sends her three neighborhoods, and she narrows them to the Deerfield Plantation area and The Farm. Deerfield is closer to the beach, but the closest beach access there has parking for golf carts only. Megan rides her bike to the beach, so she does not need a parking spot. She chooses Deerfield..."
   - Retell: Megan wanted cheap and near the beach, Deerfield's beach access has no car parking, but she bikes, so she picks Deerfield. Megan solves it herself. The problem never threatens what she wants. The owner asked for a story with "real tension, showing Chapter3's skill."
   - Wanted: the agent catches the catch, and the catch matters to her. For example: "Megan wants a house near the beach that costs less, and she wants to drive to the beach every morning. She likes a house in the Deerfield Plantation area. Her Chapter3 agent drives her to the closest beach access, and it has parking for golf carts only. The agent shows her the next one, with a pay lot, a short drive away. Megan plans on a golf cart for summer mornings and makes her offer." Check it with `tools/story-check.js`. "Golf cart" fits the owner's own advice on record (`stories.json`, golf-cart-community).

3. **Low.** Pine Lakes: the table gives "About $95 for a house", and the text says "Homes for sale on the older streets near the club are listed with no HOA fee." A reader asks which is true. Wanted: "The older streets near the club, outside the outlined parts, have no HOA fee."
4. **Low.** Two new-home prices sit below the typical price with no reason given: Wild Wing "About $600,000" in the table, then "Builders sold new homes here for about $430,000"; Waterbridge "About $630,000", then new homes "about $555,000". A reader expects new to cost more. Name the group in the sentence ("in its newest sections, builders sold smaller new houses for about...") if a verified row says so, or cut the new-home price and keep "builders are selling new homes here."
5. **Low.** Exact figures where a round one helps: "townhomes $398,000" (say "about $400,000"), "Builders sold new townhomes here for $300,000 to $356,000" ("about $300,000 to $355,000"), "About 415 homes and lots" ("about 400").
6. **Low.** The 55+ sentence: "Myrtle Trace requires it in every home." The gated page says "Myrtle Trace and Seasons at Prince Creek West require it in every home", and the Seasons ledger (2026-10-a, row 5) verifies that. Use the gated sentence here, so the two pages read the same.
7. **Low.** "If no one in your home is 55 yet, skip this part." It talks about the page. Cut it; the heading already says who the section is for.
8. **Low.** "We chose these 15 by what each one has: home prices, the HOA fee, the drive to the beach, golf and pools." Method. Cut it; keep the HOA definition that follows.
9. **Low.** "Is Myrtle Beach a good place to live?" is in PLAN.md's searches and is still not in the FAQ. One plain answer would do it, describing what the area has (beach, prices, drive times), never who lives there.
