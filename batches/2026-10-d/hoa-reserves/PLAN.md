## The reader and their job

A buyer looking at a condo on the Grand Strand, often from New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Florida or Maryland (row 26), often a second-home buyer or an investor. The lender, the listing or a friend said "check the reserves". They have a budget or a reserve study in hand, or will soon, and cannot tell whether $400,000 saved is a lot. Second reader: an owner whose board raised dues or talked about a special assessment. Third: a seller whose buyer's lender wants the budget.

Their job, in one verb: **judge** (is this building's savings enough for its age, and will my loan close). Then **decide** (offer, negotiate, or walk).

What they want first: a yardstick and the local rule. What they already know: little. "Percent funded", "fully funded balance", "baseline funding", "horizontal property regime" and "Fannie Mae" are new words. What worries them: a five-figure bill after closing, a loan that will not close, a board that put off the roof. What they ask next: what the lender checks, what the study should contain, how old is too old, and what we would do.

## The deciding fact (checked first)

**South Carolina law does not require a condo or an HOA to keep reserves, fund them at any level, or have a reserve study** (row 1). Read in full on 2026-11-01: the Horizontal Property Act uses "reserve" for money once, in the repair section (row 3); the HOA Act and the Nonprofit Corporation Act have no reserve rule; the bylaws a condo must have do not include one. That makes the page's promise real: no law makes the building save, so the building's own study, budget and balance sheet are the only measure, and reading them is the service. It also sets up the contrast every out-of-state buyer needs (rows 18 to 23).

The second deciding fact: **the lender's test reads one budget line, not the balance** (rows 12, 31). A building can put 10 percent of its budget into reserves and still be 26 percent funded. Nobody else on the first page of results says this with a worked number.

## The shape

Kind: **decision guide built on a rule and a yardstick, with a calculator** (CRAFT 3; "Page shapes" sections on law explainers, calculators and decision guides). Main relations: *condition* (does a rule apply here; will the loan close) and *amount* (how much is enough). Dominant shape: the rule in one sentence, then the yardstick as a banded table and a gauge, then one worked example the reader can follow, then the lender's test as a tool, then why it matters more here, then the rule elsewhere, then what we read, as steps.

It is not a list of places and not market data. The local findings carry it: South Carolina's silence (row 1), our count of how old the buildings for sale are, elevator and oceanfront buildings most of all (rows 24, 25), and the worked example that shows a building can pass the lender's test while weak (row 31).

Wrong-shape checks:
- The first section is the answer, not history or statute text. Statute names go in the sources line.
- The worked example comes before the lender section, so the reader has the yardstick before the trap.
- The page never tells the reader to read the study or ask the HOA. It says what we read and what it means.
- Each figure is stated once. The 10 and 15 percent appear in the key-facts row and in section 4 only.
- Special assessments get one line and a link; the sibling owns them.
