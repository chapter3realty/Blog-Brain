## The reader and their job

A Horry County homeowner who plans to list in the next few months. The house is most likely 15 to 30 years old (half the homes here were built in 2003 or earlier). Many live here; many are out of state and have not seen the house in a while. They have heard that "buyers can't get insurance on an old roof" and that "you never get back what you spend on a kitchen". They do not know what a four-point inspection or a CL-100 is until a buyer's agent asks for one. They fear two things: spending money that does not come back, and a deal falling apart in the last two weeks over a roof, a heat pump or termites.

**Job, in one verb: decide** (what to fix, what to leave, and whether to inspect first). Kind of page: decision guide with a checklist inside it (`reports/Page shapes by kind of page.md` sections 8 and 9). The main relation in the content is **condition**: which item, at which age, trips which gatekeeper (the buyer's lender, the buyer's insurer, the termite report). One dominant shape: a worked list of items against the people who check them, then the cheap fixes and the projects to skip.

## The deciding fact for the reader

A buyer's lender and insurer, not taste, decide which repairs matter. FHA's appraiser must flag a roof with less than two years of life and a heat or air system that does not work (rows 7, 8). The four-point form insurers use asks the age of the roof, the heating and air, the water heater and the electrical panel (row 12). And homes here are at that age: **a house built in 2003, the middle year in Horry County, is 23 years old; if its roof, outdoor unit and water heater are original, each is past HUD's table life of 20, 15 and 12 years** (rows 1, 4, 5). About 1 in 6 houses for sale built in 2005 or earlier already advertise a new roof (row 24). Everything else is cheap and cosmetic, and big remodels return 50 to 60 percent of their cost (rows 21, 22).

**5. The CL-100: the termite and moisture report buyers order.** What it is in one sentence (South Carolina's official wood infestation report, by a licensed pest inspector, for a sale or a loan). What it checks (crawlspace, attic, moisture readings, rot below the floor, the bottoms of doors, steps and porch posts). The 20 percent moisture line and the usual fix (a plastic vapor barrier, more vents). Repaired damage is still listed. It is not a warranty and no one has to fix what it finds; the regulation sets no expiry date. Then our count: about 4 in 10 houses for sale built in 1985 or earlier list a crawlspace, against about 1 in 35 built 2006 to 2015 (row 25), so older houses are where the moisture line comes up. Photo: termite damage at the bottom of siding by a foundation vent (candidate 2), or the partner's crawlspace shot. Proves: rows 9, 14, 15, 16, 25.
- **Offer box**: headline "Have a CL-100 or an inspection report you do not understand?" Button: "Send us the report" → /contact/.

**9. Should you get your own inspection first, and fix, credit or sell as is?** Two parts.
- Pre-listing inspection: a two-column plain list under a thin rule. For: no surprises in the last two weeks; you choose who fixes it and when; you can price it in. Against: what you learn, you must disclose (27-50-65; the plain reading in row 18, with "ask a South Carolina real estate attorney about a specific report"); the buyer will still order their own (27-50-80). Link /sell/seller-disclosure/ for the form.
- Then the one chooser, three cards: **Fix it before closing** (when the lender requires it: FHA roof, a C6 rating; rows 7, 10), **Give a credit** (FHA caps seller-paid costs at 6 percent of the price and only for closing costs; a repair escrow only if the house is livable and safe; row 11), **Sell as is** (SC law allows it; you still fill in the disclosure; a buyer with an FHA loan may not be able to buy it; link /sell/cash-offer/; rows 19, 7). Proves: rows 7, 10, 11, 17 to 19.
